1. Mathematical Models

What a statistical model is, why we build one, and how it is tested and improved.

A mathematical model is a simplified description of a real-world situation, written in mathematical terms. In statistics a model is usually a probability distribution or an equation linking two variables.

Key Facts — The Models in S1

Discrete uniform distribution: $P(X=x)=\dfrac1n$ for $x=1,2,\dots,n$, for outcomes that are equally likely (Topic 7).

Normal distribution: $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, for continuous measurements that cluster symmetrically about a mean (Topic 8).

Regression line: $y=a+bx$, for two variables with a linear relationship (Topic 6).

Why Use a Model?

  • It simplifies a complicated real situation so that it can be analysed.
  • It is quicker and cheaper than collecting data from the whole real situation.
  • It lets you make predictions about what is likely to happen.
  • It improves understanding: you can see how changing one quantity affects another.

Limitations

  • A model simplifies, so it does not include every feature of the real situation.
  • It may work only under certain conditions, or over a limited range of values.
  • Its predictions are only as good as the assumptions it is built on.

The Modelling Cycle

A model is not written once and trusted for ever. It is tested against real data and improved.

Stages 2 to 6 repeat until the model describes the data well enough.
  1. Observe a real-world problem.
  2. Devise a mathematical model, stating the assumptions it makes.
  3. Predict: use the model to predict what should happen.
  4. Collect experimental data from the real situation.
  5. Compare the data with the predictions. Statistical tests can judge how well they agree (the tests themselves are not part of S1).
  6. Refine the model if the agreement is poor, then predict and compare again.
Tip

Answer modelling questions in context. "It is quicker" is vague; "it is quicker and cheaper than timing every customer in the shop" names the situation, so it answers the question that was asked.

Common Mistake

Treating a model's prediction as exact. A model gives an estimate, and it can even allow impossible values: a Normal model for heights gives a tiny probability to negative heights. Write "the model predicts" or "an estimate is", not "the value will be".

2. Measures of Location & Spread

One number for the centre, one for the spread, and the exact rules S1 uses to find them.

Large data sets are often grouped into classes. For a class, the class boundaries are the smallest and largest values that could belong to it, the class width is the difference between the boundaries, and the midpoint is their mean.

Measures of Location

Formula — Mean

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$\bar x =\dfrac{\sum x}{n} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \bar x =\dfrac{\sum fx}{\sum f}$$

For grouped data, use each class midpoint as $x$. The result is then an estimate, because the individual values are unknown.

  • The mode (or modal class) is the value (or class) that occurs most often.
  • The median, $Q_2$, is the middle value when the data are in order.
  • The mean uses every value, so extreme values pull it. The median is not affected by extreme values.

Quartiles of Discrete Data

The lower quartile $Q_1$, the median $Q_2$ and the upper quartile $Q_3$ split the ordered data into four equal parts.

Rule — Quartiles of a List of $n$ Values

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet). Put the data in order, then work out

$$\dfrac n4\ \text{ for }Q_1, \qquad \dfrac n2\ \text{ for }Q_2, \qquad \dfrac{3n}4\ \text{ for }Q_3.$$

Whole number: take the mean of that value and the next one.

Not a whole number: round up, and take that value.

Common Mistake

Using an $\frac{n+1}{4}$ rule. You may have found quartiles at IGCSE from the positions $\frac{n+1}{4}$, $\frac{n+1}{2}$ and $\frac{3(n+1)}{4}$. S1 uses $\frac n4$, $\frac n2$ and $\frac{3n}{4}$ with the rule above, and the two methods can give different answers.

Grouped Data: Interpolation

In a grouped table the individual values are lost, so quartiles are estimated by interpolation: assume the values are spread evenly through the class.

Formula — Interpolation

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$Q =L+\dfrac{k-F}{f}\times w$$

$k$ is the position: $\frac n4$, $\frac n2$ or $\frac{3n}4$ for the quartiles, and $\frac{p}{100}\times n$ for the $p$th percentile. Do not round $k$.

$L$ is the lower class boundary of the class containing position $k$, $F$ is the cumulative frequency before that class, $f$ is the frequency of the class and $w$ is its width.

Tip — Class Boundaries

When there are gaps between classes, such as 10–14 and 15–19 for times measured to the nearest minute, the boundaries are half-way across each gap: 9.5, 14.5, 19.5. When the classes are written as $10\leq t\lt15$, the boundaries are the stated values, 10 and 15.

The variance measures how far the values are from the mean, on average, and uses every value. The standard deviation is its square root, so it has the same units as the data.

Formula — Variance and Standard Deviation

Learn this (the booklet does not give the variance of a data set):

$$\sigma^2 =\dfrac{\sum(x-\bar x)^2}{n} =\dfrac{\sum x^2}{n}-\left(\dfrac{\sum x}{n}\right)^2$$

$$\sigma^2 =\dfrac{\sum fx^2}{\sum f}-\left(\dfrac{\sum fx}{\sum f}\right)^2 \qquad \sigma =\sqrt{\text{variance}}$$

In the formulae booklet (under Correlation and regression):

$$S_{xx} =\sum(x-\bar x)^2 =\sum x^2-\dfrac{(\sum x)^2}{n}, \qquad \text{so} \quad \sigma^2 =\dfrac{S_{xx}}{n}$$

In words: the variance is "the mean of the squares minus the square of the mean". For the values 4, 7, 7, 10, 12 the mean is $8$ and $\sum x^2=358$, so $\sigma^2=\dfrac{358}{5}-8^2=7.6$.

Common Mistake

Dividing by $n-1$. In S1 the variance has divisor $n$. Many calculators show two standard deviations: use $\sigma_x$, not $s_x$. Also keep $\sum x^2$ (square, then add) separate from $(\sum x)^2$ (add, then square).

3. Coding

Making awkward numbers simple before you calculate, then undoing the code correctly.

Coding replaces each data value $x$ by a simpler value $y$, using a linear rule. You calculate with the easy $y$ values, then convert the answers back.

Formula — Coding $y=\dfrac{x-a}{b}$

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet). For $b\gt0$:

$$\bar y =\dfrac{\bar x-a}{b} \qquad \sigma_y =\dfrac{\sigma_x}{b}$$

Decoding:

$$\bar x =b\,\bar y+a \qquad \sigma_x =b\,\sigma_y$$

What is done to every valueMeanStandard deviation
Add or subtract a constantThe same constant is added or subtracted.Unchanged.
Multiply or divide by a positive constantMultiplied or divided by the constant.Multiplied or divided by the constant.

Adding a constant slides every value along by the same amount, so the centre moves but the spread does not. Multiplying stretches the data, so both the centre and the spread change.

Tip

To decode, rearrange the code to make $x$ the subject, such as $x=by+a$. The mean follows the whole rule. The standard deviation uses only the multiplier $b$, and the variance uses $b^2$.

Common Mistake

Adding $a$ back to the standard deviation when decoding. A shift does not change how spread out the data are, so $\sigma_x=b\,\sigma_y$ with nothing added.

Worked Example 5 Decoding a mean and standard deviation

The masses, $x$ grams, of a sample of letters are coded using $y=\dfrac{x-50}{4}$. The coded values have mean $3.6$ and standard deviation $1.25$. Find the mean and the standard deviation of the masses.

1
Rearrange the code
$y=\dfrac{x-50}{4}\Rightarrow x=4y+50$
2
Mean
The mean follows the whole rule: $\bar x=4(3.6)+50=64.4$
3
Standard deviation
Only the multiplier matters: $\sigma_x=4(1.25)=5$
Mean $=64.4$ g  ·  standard deviation $=5$ g
Worked Example 6 Coding with summary statistics

The heights, $x$ cm, of 20 young trees are coded using $y=\dfrac{x-120}{5}$. The coded data give $\sum y=46$ and $\sum y^2=134.6$. Find the mean and the standard deviation of the heights.

4. Representations of Data

Histograms, box plots and stem-and-leaf diagrams, and how to read shape, outliers and comparisons from them.

Histograms

A histogram displays grouped continuous data. There are no gaps between the bars, and the area of each bar is proportional to the frequency of its class. That is what allows the classes to have different widths.

Formula — Frequency Density

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$\text{frequency density} =\dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}$$

$$\text{area of bar} =k\times\text{frequency}$$

When the height is the frequency density, $k=1$ and the area equals the frequency. Class widths come from the class boundaries.

Worked Example 8 Working backwards from a histogram

A histogram shows the times, $t$ minutes, that customers spent in a shop. The bar for $10\leq t\lt15$ is 2 cm wide and 6 cm high, and represents 24 customers.

(a) The bar for $15\leq t\lt25$ is 4 cm wide and 3.5 cm high. Find the number of customers in this class.

(b) The class $25\leq t\lt40$ contains 18 customers. Find the width and the height of its bar.

1
Find what 1 cm² represents
The known bar has area $2\times6=12$ cm² for 24 customers, so 1 cm² represents $\dfrac{24}{12}=2$ customers.
2
(a) Use the area
Area $=4\times3.5=14$ cm², so the frequency is $14\times2=28$.
3
(b) Width
A class of width 5 minutes is drawn 2 cm wide, so a class of width 15 minutes is 6 cm wide.
4
(b) Height
18 customers need an area of $\dfrac{18}{2}=9$ cm², so the height is $\dfrac{9}{6}=1.5$ cm.
(a) 28 customers  ·  (b) 6 cm wide and 1.5 cm high

Outliers

An outlier is an extreme value that lies well outside the overall pattern of the data. The rule for deciding what counts as an outlier is always given in the question.

Rule — A Common Outlier Test

Given in the question (not in the formulae booklet). A value $x$ is an outlier if

$$x\lt Q_1-k(Q_3-Q_1) \qquad \text{or} \qquad x\gt Q_3+k(Q_3-Q_1)$$

The value of $k$ is stated in the question. It is often $1.5$.

Tip

Use the rule exactly as the question states it. Do not assume $k=1.5$, and do not assume the rule is based on the quartiles.

Box Plots

A box plot shows the quartiles, the extremes and any outliers on a single scale.

  • The box runs from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, with a line across it at the median $Q_2$.
  • Each outlier is marked with a cross.
  • Each whisker ends at the most extreme value that is not an outlier.
  • Always draw the box plot against a labelled scale.

Stem-and-Leaf Diagrams

A stem-and-leaf diagram keeps every data value while showing the shape of the distribution.

  • Each value is split into a stem (for example the tens digit) and a leaf (the units digit).
  • The leaves are written in order, one digit each, and the diagram needs a key.
  • A back-to-back diagram shows two data sets on one stem. The leaves of both sets get larger as they move away from the stem.
  • Because the data are in order, the median and quartiles can be counted straight from the diagram.

Worked Example 10 Describing skewness

The waiting times, in minutes, at a clinic have $Q_1=18$, $Q_2=22$, $Q_3=31$, mean $25.4$ and standard deviation $8.5$.

(a) Use the quartiles to describe the skewness. (b) Use the mean and median to describe the skewness. (c) Evaluate $\dfrac{3(\text{mean}-\text{median})}{\text{standard deviation}}$ and comment.

1
(a) Quartiles
$Q_2-Q_1=22-18=4$ and $Q_3-Q_2=31-22=9$. Since $Q_3-Q_2\gt Q_2-Q_1$, the upper half is more stretched out: positive skew.
2
(b) Mean and median
mean $-$ median $=25.4-22=3.4\gt0$. The mean is greater than the median: positive skew.
3
(c) Evaluate the measure
$\dfrac{3(25.4-22)}{8.5}=1.2$, which is positive: positive skew.
All three methods show positive skew: most waits are short, with a tail of long waits.

Comparing Distributions

To compare two data sets, compare one measure of location and one measure of spread, and say what each comparison means in context.

  • Use the median with the interquartile range, or the mean with the standard deviation. Do not mix the pairs.
  • The median and interquartile range are the better choice when there are outliers or the data are skewed.

Unit B · Probability

5. Probability

Venn diagrams, tree diagrams and the laws that connect "or", "and" and "given".

Vocabulary and Notation

  • An experiment is a repeatable process that produces outcomes.
  • The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes.
  • An event is a set of one or more outcomes. Its probability is between 0 and 1.
  • When the outcomes are equally likely, $P(A)=\dfrac{\text{number of outcomes in }A}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$.
Intersection $A\cap B$
$A$ and $B$ both happen
Union $A\cup B$
$A$ or $B$, or both, happen
Complement $A'$
$A$ does not happen
Conditional $P(A\mid B)$
probability of $A$ given that $B$ has happened
Formula — Probability Laws

In the formulae booklet:

$$P(A\cup B) =P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$

$$P(A\cap B) =P(A)\,P(B\mid A)$$

$$P(A\mid B) =\dfrac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)} {P(B\mid A)\,P(A) + P(B\mid A')\,P(A')}$$

Learn this (not in the booklet):

$$P(A') =1-P(A) \qquad \qquad P(A\mid B) =\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$$

The last formula is the booklet's multiplication law rearranged, with the letters swapped.

Venn Diagrams

A Venn diagram shows events as overlapping regions inside a rectangle that represents the whole sample space. The numbers in all the regions add up to 1 (for probabilities) or to the total frequency.

Tip

Fill a Venn diagram from the middle outwards. Start with the intersection, then subtract it to get the "only" regions, and find the region outside the circles last.

Worked Example 12 Two-event Venn diagram

Events $A$ and $B$ have $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.4$ and $P(A\cap B)=0.15$.

(a) Draw a Venn diagram. Find (b) $P(A\cup B)$, (c) the probability that neither event happens, (d) $P(A\mid B)$.

1

Mutually Exclusive and Independent Events

Mutually exclusive
The events cannot both happen. $$P(A\cap B) =0$$ so $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$. The circles do not overlap.
Independent
One event happening does not change the probability of the other. $$P(A\cap B) =P(A)\,P(B)$$ Equivalently $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ and $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$.

The independence results must be learnt: they are not in the formulae booklet.

Common Mistake

Mixing up "mutually exclusive" and "independent". They are different ideas. If two events with non-zero probabilities are mutually exclusive, then knowing one has happened makes the other impossible, so they are certainly not independent.

Worked Example 13 Testing for independence

Events $C$ and $D$ have $P(C)=0.6$, $P(D)=0.35$ and $P(C\cup D)=0.74$. Determine whether $C$ and $D$ are independent.

1
Find the intersection
Rearranging the addition law, $P(C\cap D)=P(C)+P(D)-P(C\cup D)=0.6+0.35-0.74=0.21$
2
Find the product
$P(C)\times P(D)=0.6\times0.35=0.21$
3
Compare
$P(C\cap D)=P(C)\,P(D)$, so the events are independent. (They are not mutually exclusive, because $P(C\cap D)\neq0$.)
$P(C\cap D)=0.21=P(C)\,P(D)$, so $C$ and $D$ are independent.

Tree Diagrams

A tree diagram sets out an experiment that happens in stages.

  • Multiply along the branches to get the probability of a route.
  • Add the probabilities of different routes that give the same event.
  • The branches at each point add up to 1.
  • Second-stage branches are conditional probabilities. Without replacement, the numbers change after the first pick; with replacement, they stay the same.

Assuming $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ are the same. They have the same numerator, $P(A\cap B)$, but different denominators. Also, without replacement, remember that the total drops by one for the second pick.

6. Correlation & Regression

Measuring how strongly two variables move together, and fitting the best straight line through the data.
↤ Builds On

P1 — Lines covers the straight line $y=mx+c$. A regression line $y=a+bx$ is the same idea with the letters rearranged: $b$ is the gradient and $a$ is the $y$-intercept.

Scatter Diagrams and Correlation

Bivariate data are pairs of values $(x,y)$, one pair for each item. A scatter diagram plots each pair as a point. Correlation describes the linear relationship between the two variables.

The Product Moment Correlation Coefficient

The product moment correlation coefficient (PMCC), $r$, measures the strength of the linear correlation on a scale from $-1$ to $1$.

Formula — Summary Statistics and $r$

In the formulae booklet:

$$S_{xx} =\sum x^2-\dfrac{(\sum x)^2}{n} \qquad S_{yy} =\sum y^2-\dfrac{(\sum y)^2}{n}$$

$$S_{xy} =\sum xy-\dfrac{\sum x\sum y}{n}$$

$$r =\dfrac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}}$$

Value of $r$Meaning
$r=1$Perfect positive linear correlation: every point lies on a line with positive gradient.
$r$ close to $1$Strong positive linear correlation.
$r$ close to $0$Little or no linear correlation.
$r$ close to $-1$Strong negative linear correlation.
$r=-1$Perfect negative linear correlation: every point lies on a line with negative gradient.

Limitations. Correlation does not prove causation: two variables can rise together because of a third factor, or by coincidence. Also, $r$ only measures linear association, so two variables can have $r$ close to 0 and still be related by a curve.

The Regression Line of $y$ on $x$

The least squares regression line is the straight line that fits the data best: it makes the sum of the squares of the vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible.

  • The explanatory (independent) variable, $x$, is the one that is set or that changes first.
  • The response (dependent) variable, $y$, is the one that is measured as a result.
Formula — Regression Line

In the formulae booklet:

$$y =a+bx \qquad \text{where} \qquad b =\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} \quad \text{and} \quad a =\bar y-b\bar x$$

The line always passes through the mean point $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

Interpreting $b$
The change in $y$ for each increase of 1 unit in $x$.
Interpreting $a$
The value of $y$ when $x=0$. It only has meaning if $x=0$ is sensible and close to the data.
Interpolation
Estimating inside the range of the $x$ data. Usually reliable.
Extrapolation
Estimating outside the range of the $x$ data. Unreliable: the pattern may not continue.

7. Discrete Random Variables

Variables whose values come with probabilities: tables, expectations, and the simplest model of all.

A random variable $X$ is a variable whose value depends on the outcome of an experiment. It is discrete if it can take only certain separate values. The capital letter $X$ is the variable, and the small letter $x$ is a value it can take.

Probability Distributions

A probability distribution lists every value of $X$ with its probability, as a table or as a probability function $p(x)=P(X=x)$. The probabilities always add up to 1.

Expectation and Variance

The expected value $E(X)$ is the mean of $X$: the long-run average if the experiment is repeated many times. $\text{Var}(X)$ measures the spread of $X$ about that mean.

Formula — Discrete Random Variables

In the formulae booklet:

$$E(X) =\mu =\sum x\,P(X =x)$$

$$\text{Var}(X) =\sigma^2 =\sum x^2\,P(X =x)-\mu^2$$

$$E(g(X)) =\sum g(x)\,P(X =x)$$

Learn this (not in the booklet):

$$\sum P(X =x) =1 \qquad \qquad F(x_0) =P(X\leq x_0)$$

$$E(aX+b) =aE(X)+b \qquad \qquad \text{Var}(aX+b) =a^2\,\text{Var}(X)$$

Taking $g(X)=X^2$ in the third booklet formula gives $E(X^2)=\sum x^2\,P(X=x)$, so the variance is usually written $\text{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2$.

Stopping at $E(X^2)$ and calling it the variance, or squaring the wrong thing. $E(X^2)$ squares the values first and then averages. $[E(X)]^2$ averages first and then squares. The variance is the first minus the second.

The Cumulative Distribution Function

The cumulative distribution function $F(x)=P(X\leq x)$ is a running total of the probabilities.

  • $F$ at the largest value of $X$ is always 1.
  • To recover a single probability, subtract: $P(X=x)=F(x)-F(\text{previous value})$.
  • $P(X\gt x)=1-F(x)$.

The Discrete Uniform Distribution

A random variable has a discrete uniform distribution when all of its values are equally likely. It is the natural model for a fair dice, a fair spinner or a random digit.

Formula — Discrete Uniform on $1,2,\dots,n$

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$P(X =x) =\dfrac1n \qquad E(X) =\dfrac{n+1}{2} \qquad \text{Var}(X) =\dfrac{n^2-1}{12}$$

These two results need the values to be exactly $1,2,\dots,n$. For any other set of equally likely values, find $E(X)$ and $\text{Var}(X)$ from the definitions.

8. The Normal Distribution

The bell curve, the two tables in the booklet, and working backwards from a probability.

The Normal distribution is a model for a continuous random variable, such as a height, a mass or a time. $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ means that $X$ is Normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, so its standard deviation is $\sigma$.

Shape and Properties

  • The curve is bell-shaped and symmetrical about the mean $\mu$, so mean $=$ median $=$ mode.
  • Probability is area under the curve, and the total area is 1.
  • The curve never touches the horizontal axis, but the area far from the mean is tiny: about $68\%$ of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, $95\%$ within two and $99.7\%$ within three.
  • $P(X=a)=0$ for any single value $a$, so $P(X\lt a)$ and $P(X\leq a)$ are equal.

Standardising and the Table of $\Phi(z)$

Every Normal distribution can be converted to the standard Normal $Z\sim N(0,1)$. The booklet tabulates $\Phi(z)=P(Z\lt z)$.

Formula — Standardising

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$Z =\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma} \qquad \text{so} \qquad P(X\lt a) =\Phi\!\left(\dfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)$$

$$P(Z\gt z) =1-\Phi(z) \qquad \Phi(-z) =1-\Phi(z) \qquad P(a\lt Z\lt b) =\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)$$

In the formulae booklet: the table of $\Phi(z)$ and the table of percentage points.

The curve is symmetrical, so the two shaded tails have equal areas: $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$.

For example, the table gives $\Phi(1.25)=0.8944$. So $P(Z\gt1.25)=1-0.8944=0.1056$ and, by symmetry, $\Phi(-1.25)=0.1056$ as well.

Tip — Using the Table

The table lists $z$ to 2 decimal places, so round your $z$ value to 2 decimal places before looking it up. The specification says interpolation is not necessary. Above $z=2.00$ the table lists $z$ in larger steps, so use the closest listed value or a calculator. Always sketch the curve and shade the area you want: it shows at once whether the answer should be more or less than $0.5$.

Worked Example 24 Finding a value from a probability

The lifetimes of a brand of battery, $X$ hours, are modelled by $X\sim N(48,5^2)$.

(a) The longest-lasting 5% of batteries last more than $x$ hours. Find $x$. (b) The shortest-lived 10% of batteries last less than $w$ hours. Find $w$.

1
(a) Find z
$P(X\gt x)=0.05$ is an upper tail, so the percentage points table gives $z=1.6449$.
2
(a) Un-standardise
$\dfrac{x-48}{5}=1.6449\Rightarrow x=48+1.6449\times5=56.2245$
3
(b) Find z
$P(X\lt w)=0.10$ is a lower tail, so $z=-1.2816$.
4
(b) Un-standardise
$\dfrac{w-48}{5}=-1.2816\Rightarrow w=48-1.2816\times5=41.592$
(a) $x=56.2$ hours  ·  (b) $w=41.6$ hours (3 s.f.)
Worked Example 24(a). The top 5% lies to the right of $x=56.2$.

Finding $\mu$ or $\sigma$

If $\mu$ or $\sigma$ is unknown, turn each given probability into a $z$ value and write $\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}=z$. One unknown needs one equation. Two unknowns need two probabilities and give simultaneous equations.

Common Mistake

Losing the sign of $z$. A value with less than half the distribution below it is below the mean, so its $z$ value is negative: $P(X\lt a)=0.01$ gives $z=-2.3263$, not $+2.3263$. Also check which number is which in $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $N(50,16)$ has $\sigma=4$, not 16.

Worked Example 25 Finding $\mu$

The volume of juice in a carton, $X$ ml, is modelled by $X\sim N(\mu,3^2)$. Only 1% of cartons contain less than 250 ml. Find $\mu$.

1
Find z
$P(X\lt250)=0.01$ is a lower tail. The table gives $2.3263$ for $p=0.01$, so $z=-2.3263$.
2
Form the equation
$\dfrac{250-\mu}{3}=-2.3263$
3
Solve
$\mu=250+2.3263\times3=250+6.9789=256.9789$
$\mu=257$ ml (3 s.f.)
Worked Example 26 Finding $\mu$ and $\sigma$

The time for a journey, $X$ minutes, is modelled by $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. 10% of journeys take less than 30 minutes and 5% take more than 55 minutes. Find $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

1
Lower tail
$P(X\lt30)=0.10$ gives $z=-1.2816$: $\dfrac{30-\mu}{\sigma}=-1.2816\Rightarrow30-\mu=-1.2816\sigma$
2
Upper tail
$P(X\gt55)=0.05$ gives $z=1.6449$: $\dfrac{55-\mu}{\sigma}=1.6449\Rightarrow55-\mu=1.6449\sigma$
3
Subtract the equations
$(55-\mu)-(30-\mu)=1.6449\sigma+1.2816\sigma\Rightarrow25=2.9265\sigma$, so $\sigma=8.5426\ldots$
4
Back-substitute
$\mu=30+1.2816\sigma=30+10.948\ldots=40.948\ldots$
$\mu=40.9$ minutes  ·  $\sigma=8.54$ minutes (3 s.f.)
