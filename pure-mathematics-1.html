1. Surds

Irrational roots, and the four moves that let you manipulate them like ordinary numbers.

A surd is a root, usually a square root, that can't be simplified to a whole number, so it stays written in root form to keep the answer exact.

Core Rules

Formula — Surd Rules

$$\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}=\sqrt{ab}$$

$$\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{b}}$$

$$a\sqrt{b}+c\sqrt{b}=(a+c)\sqrt{b}$$

$$a\sqrt{b}-c\sqrt{b}=(a-c)\sqrt{b}$$

Tip

Only like surds (same number under the root) can be added or subtracted — simplify each surd first to check whether they secretly match.

Worked Example Expanding surds

Expand and simplify $\sqrt{5}(1-2\sqrt5)$.

1
Expand
$\sqrt5 \times 1 - \sqrt5\times2\sqrt5 = \sqrt5 - 2(\sqrt5\times\sqrt5)$
2
Simplify
$\sqrt5\times\sqrt5 = 5$, so this becomes $\sqrt5 - 2(5)$
$\sqrt5 - 10$

Rationalising the Denominator

Rationalising means removing a surd from the denominator, so the fraction is written in a standard, comparable form.

Form $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt a}$
Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt A$
Form $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt a \pm \sqrt b}$
Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $\sqrt A \mp \sqrt B$
Worked Example Conjugate rationalising

Rationalise $\dfrac{5}{2+\sqrt7}$.

1
Conjugate
Multiply top and bottom by $2-\sqrt7$
2
Denominator
$(2+\sqrt7)(2-\sqrt7) = 4 - 7 = -3$
3
Numerator
$5(2-\sqrt7) = 10-5\sqrt7$
$\dfrac{10-5\sqrt7}{-3} = \dfrac{5\sqrt7-10}{3}$

2. Simultaneous Equations

Finding where two graphs meet: linear-linear, or linear-nonlinear.

Solving simultaneous equations means finding the intersection point(s) of two graphs: two lines (linear-linear), or a line and a curve/circle (linear-nonlinear).

Linear – Linear

Use elimination or substitution. Show the equation you eliminate or substitute and the resulting value: these algebraic steps are the evidence for method marks.

Worked Example Elimination

Solve $3x+2y=8$ and $2x+5y=-2$.

1
Match coeffs
$\times5$: $15x+10y=40$    $\times2$: $4x+10y=-4$
2
Subtract
$11x = 44 \Rightarrow x=4$
3
Back-substitute
$3(4)+2y=8 \Rightarrow y=-2$
$(x,y)=(4,-2)$

Linear – Nonlinear

  1. Make $x$ or $y$ the subject in the linear equation (easiest when its coefficient is $+1$ or $-1$).
  2. Substitute into the nonlinear equation.
  3. Solve the resulting quadratic by factorisation, completing the square or the quadratic formula, then substitute each root back to find the matching coordinate.
Common Mistake

Substituting the nonlinear equation into the linear one (instead of the reverse) usually makes the algebra far messier — so always eliminate using the linear equation first.

Worked Example Line meets curve

Solve $y = x+1$ and $y = x^2-x-1$.

1
Substitute
$x+1 = x^2-x-1$
2
Rearrange
$x^2-2x-2=0$
3
Solve
$x = \dfrac{2\pm\sqrt{4+8}}{2} = 1\pm\sqrt3$
4
Back-substitute
If $x=1+\sqrt3$, then $y=2+\sqrt3$; if $x=1-\sqrt3$, then $y=2-\sqrt3$.
$(1+\sqrt3, 2+\sqrt3)$ and $(1-\sqrt3, 2-\sqrt3)$

3. Quadratics

Solving $ax^2+bx+c=0$, and rewriting it to reveal the turning point.

A quadratic equation has the form $ax^2+bx+c=0$ where $a\neq0$. It can have two real roots, one repeated root, or no real roots.

Choosing a Method

MethodBest used whenWhat must be shown
FactorisationThe factors can be found exactly and quicklyThe two factors and the zero-product step
Completing the squareThe vertex or range is needed, or the expression completes neatlyThe completed-square form before solving
Quadratic formulaFactorisation is not convenientCorrect values of $a,b,c$ substituted into the formula
Calculator solverA question permits numerical rootsThe quadratic equation being solved and suitably rounded roots
Before Solving

Rearrange the equation into $ax^2+bx+c=0$. For factorisation, use the fact that if $pq=0$, then $p=0$ or $q=0$.

Worked Example Factorising and reading the graph

For $y=x^2-4x-5$, solve $y=0$ and state the intercepts, vertex and shape of the graph.

1
Factorise and solve
$x^2-4x-5=(x-5)(x+1)$. Hence $(x-5)(x+1)=0$, so $x=5$ or $x=-1$.
2
Find the intercepts
The $x$-intercepts are $(5,0)$ and $(-1,0)$. At $x=0$, $y=-5$, so the $y$-intercept is $(0,-5)$.
3
Find the vertex
$x^2-4x-5=(x-2)^2-9$, so the vertex is $(2,-9)$.
4
State the shape
The coefficient of $x^2$ is positive, so the parabola opens upwards and the vertex is a minimum.
Roots $-1,5$; intercepts $(-1,0),(5,0),(0,-5)$; minimum at $(2,-9)$.

Quadratic Formula

Formula

$$x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$$

Completing the Square

Formula

$$a\left(x+\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \dfrac{b^2}{4a} + c$$

This form shows the turning point directly at $\left(-\dfrac{b}{2a},\; c-\dfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)$: crucial for sketching and for minimum/maximum problems.

Worked Example Completing the square

Write $2x^2-8x+3$ in completed-square form.

1
Factor out $a$
$2(x^2-4x)+3$
2
Halve, square
$2\big[(x-2)^2-4\big]+3$
3
Simplify
$2(x-2)^2-8+3 = 2(x-2)^2-5$
$2(x-2)^2-5$

This method returns in P2 — Circles, where completing the square on $x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+c=0$ is exactly how you find a circle's centre and radius.

4. Inequalities

Solving linear and quadratic inequalities · graphical interpretation · solid and dotted boundaries

Linear Inequalities

  • Solve exactly like a linear equation.
  • Keep the variable on the left-hand side where possible.
  • Multiplying or dividing by a negative number flips the inequality sign.
Worked Example Brackets and a fraction

Solve $2(x-3)\leq\dfrac{x+1}{2}$.

1
Clear the denominator
Multiply both sides by $2$: $4(x-3)\leq x+1$.
2
Expand and collect
$4x-12\leq x+1 \Rightarrow 3x\leq13$.
$x\leq\dfrac{13}{3}$

Quadratic Inequalities

Do Not Memorise “Inside” or “Outside” Blindly

The sign pattern depends on the direction of the parabola and on the inequality. Rearrange so one side is zero, find the boundary roots, then use a sketch or sign test. A calculator may check roots, but your written reasoning must identify the correct interval.

Reliable Method
  1. Rearrange into $q(x)\gt0$, $q(x)\geq0$, $q(x)\lt0$ or $q(x)\leq0$.
  2. Solve $q(x)=0$ to find the boundary values.
  3. Sketch the parabola or test one value in each interval.
  4. Include a boundary root only when the sign is $\leq$ or $\geq$.
Worked Example

Solve $x^2+5x \geq -6$.

1
Rearrange
$x^2+5x+6 \geq 0$
2
Factorise
$(x+2)(x+3)\geq0$, roots at $x=-2,\,-3$
3
Use the sign
The upward-opening quadratic is on or above the $x$-axis outside the roots. Equality includes the roots.
$x\leq-3$ or $x\geq-2$
Worked Example Negative leading coefficient

Solve $-x^2+5x-6\gt0$.

1
Factorise
$-x^2+5x-6=-(x-2)(x-3)$, so the boundary roots are $2$ and $3$.
2
Interpret the graph
The parabola opens downward and lies above the $x$-axis between its roots.
$2\lt x\lt3$

Representing Inequalities Graphically

First draw the boundary obtained by replacing the inequality sign with equality. Then decide whether the boundary belongs to the solution and which side must be shaded.

InequalityBoundary styleReason
$\lt$ or $\gt$Dotted line or curvePoints on the boundary are not included.
$\leq$ or $\geq$Solid line or curvePoints on the boundary are included.
  • Shade above $y=f(x)$ for $y\gt f(x)$ or $y\geq f(x)$.
  • Shade below $y=f(x)$ for $y\lt f(x)$ or $y\leq f(x)$.
  • For a boundary written in another form, test a simple point such as $(0,0)$ when it is not on the boundary.

Comparing a Curve and a Line

An inequality such as $x^2-2x-3\lt x+1$ asks where the curve $y=x^2-2x-3$ lies below the line $y=x+1$. Their intersection points are the boundary values.

The parabola is below the line between the two intersections, so the solution is $-1\lt x\lt4$.
Worked Example Curve versus line

Solve $x^2-2x-3\lt x+1$.

1
Find the intersections
$x^2-2x-3=x+1 \Rightarrow x^2-3x-4=0$.
2
Factorise
$(x-4)(x+1)=0$, so the boundary values are $x=-1$ and $x=4$.
3
Choose the correct region
Between the intersections the parabola lies below the line. The inequality is strict, so neither endpoint is included.
$-1\lt x\lt4$

5. The Discriminant

One expression tells you how many times a quadratic meets the x-axis, or two curves meet each other.
↦ Continues In

The same "set the discriminant to zero" logic reappears in P2 — Circles when finding the tangent to a circle from a line.

Formula

$$D = b^2-4ac$$

ValueMeaning
$D=0$One real (repeated) root: curve touches the x-axis, or two graphs are tangent
$D>0$Two real roots: curve crosses the x-axis twice, or two graphs meet at two points
$D<0$No real roots: curve doesn't touch the x-axis, or two graphs never meet
Tip — Keywords

"Tangent" or "meets exactly once" → D=0.  "Meets at two points" → D>0.  "Does not meet/intersect" → D<0.

Worked Example

Find $k$ such that $y=kx+3$ is tangent to $y=x^2+2x+5$.

1
Equate
$x^2+2x+5 = kx+3 \Rightarrow x^2+(2-k)x+2=0$
2
Tangent ⇒ D = 0
$(2-k)^2 - 8 = 0$
3
Solve
$(2-k)^2=8 \Rightarrow k-2=\pm2\sqrt2$
$k=2\pm2\sqrt2$

6. Indices

The laws that let you simplify powers without ever expanding them.
Multiplying
$a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}$
Dividing
$a^m\div a^n=a^{m-n}$
Power of a power
$(a^m)^n=a^{mn}$
Negative power
$a^{-m}=\dfrac{1}{a^m}$
Zero power
$a^0=1$
Fractional power
$a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}$
Root form
$\sqrt[m]{a}=a^{\frac1m}$
Equal bases
if $a^x=a^y$ then $x=y$
Tip

Whenever an equation has the unknown in the exponent, the goal is almost always to write both sides with the same base so you can equate the powers directly.

Worked Example

Solve $9^{x} = 27^{2-x}$.

1
Common base
$9=3^2$, $27=3^3$, so $3^{2x}=3^{3(2-x)}$
2
Equate powers
$2x = 6-3x$
3
Answer
$5x=6 \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{6}{5}$
$x=\dfrac65$
Unit B · Curves & Functions

7. Transformations of Curves

Six moves for shifting, flipping, and stretching any graph $y=f(x)$.

Changes made inside the brackets of $f(x)$ transform the curve horizontally (along $x$). Changes made outside the brackets transform it vertically (along $y$).

Transformation on $x$Transformation on $y$
$f(x\pm a)$ → translation on the x-axis$f(x)\pm a$ → translation on the y-axis
$f(-x)$ → reflection in the y-axis$-f(x)$ → reflection in the x-axis
$f(ax)$ → horizontal scale factor $1/a$$a\,f(x)$ → vertical scale factor $a$

These scale-factor statements assume $a>0$; a negative multiplier also introduces the corresponding reflection.

Tip

To solve for a point on the transformed curve, work backwards from the graph: for an X-transformation apply the OPPOSITE operation to your coordinate; for a Y-transformation apply the SAME operation.

8. Sketching Functions

Linear, quadratic, cubic and reciprocal curves: the same four questions, answered every time.

From a sketch, identify the intersections with each axis and the intervals where $f(x)$ is positive or negative. The core algebraic graph shapes for P1 are linear, quadratic, simple cubic and reciprocal curves $y=\dfrac{k}{x}$ and $y=\dfrac{k}{x^2}$, with $x\ne0$. Their single transformations are covered in Transformations of Curves.

Polynomial Manipulation up to Degree 3

Before solving or sketching, expand brackets, collect like terms and factorise where possible. For a cubic, first take out any common factor; grouping may then reveal a common linear factor.

Worked Example Factorising a cubic by grouping

Factorise fully $2x^3+x^2-8x-4$, and hence solve $2x^3+x^2-8x-4=0$.

1
Group the terms
$(2x^3+x^2)+(-8x-4)=x^2(2x+1)-4(2x+1)$.
2
Take out the common factor
$(2x+1)(x^2-4)=(2x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$.
3
Use the zero-product rule
$2x+1=0$, $x-2=0$ or $x+2=0$.
$(2x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$; $x=-2,-\dfrac12,2$.

Using Graph Intersections to Solve an Equation

To solve $f(x)=g(x)$ graphically, draw $y=f(x)$ and $y=g(x)$. The $x$-coordinates of their intersection points are the solutions; their $y$-coordinates give the corresponding common values.

Worked Example Intersections

The graphs $y=x^2$ and $y=2x+3$ are drawn on the same axes. Find their intersections and hence solve $x^2=2x+3$.

1
Equate the graph equations
$x^2=2x+3 \Rightarrow x^2-2x-3=0$.
2
Find the $x$-coordinates
$(x-3)(x+1)=0$, so $x=3$ or $x=-1$.
3
Find the common $y$-values
Using $y=x^2$, $y=9$ when $x=3$ and $y=1$ when $x=-1$.
Intersections $(-1,1)$ and $(3,9)$; solutions $x=-1,3$.

Type 1: Linear — $y=mx+c$

  1. x-intercept: set $y=0$ and solve for $x$
  2. y-intercept: set $x=0$
  3. Gradient from the sign of $m$: positive → increasing, negative → decreasing

Type 2: Quadratic — $y=ax^2+bx+c$

  1. x-intercept(s): set $y=0$ and solve. Use the discriminant to determine how many roots exist.
  2. y-intercept: set $x=0$
  3. Turning point: $x=-\dfrac{b}{2a}$, or read directly from completed-square form.
  4. Shape: $a>0$ opens upward, $a<0$ opens downward.

Type 3: Cubic — $y=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$

  1. x-intercept(s): set $y=0$ and solve, factorising where possible.
  2. y-intercept: set $x=0$
  3. Overall shape depends on the sign of $a$.
Tip

A cubic curve can have one, two (one repeated), or three real x-intercepts.

Type 4: Reciprocal — $y=\dfrac{k}{x}$ and $y=\dfrac{k}{x^2}$

For these reciprocal curves, take $k\ne0$ and $x\ne0$.

  1. No $x$-intercept: the numerator $k$ is non-zero.
  2. No $y$-intercept: the function is undefined at $x=0$.
  3. Vertical asymptote $x=0$; horizontal asymptote $y=0$.
  4. For $k>0$, $k/x$ has branches in quadrants I and III, while $k/x^2$ is positive on both sides of the $y$-axis. For $k<0$, reflect the corresponding positive-$k$ graph in the $x$-axis.
Note

Single transformation: replacing $x$ by $x-h$ shifts either graph horizontally by $h$. For $y=\dfrac{k}{x-h}$ or $y=\dfrac{k}{(x-h)^2}$, the vertical asymptote becomes $x=h$ and the horizontal asymptote remains $y=0$.

Unit C · Coordinate Geometry

9. Lines

Everything two coordinates can tell you, and how to build the equation of a line from them.

Given any two points, you can always compute the gradient, the distance between them, and their midpoint.

Gradient
$$m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$$
Distance
$$d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$$
Midpoint
$$\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$$

A straight line can be written in several equivalent forms:

FormUse
$y=mx+c$Shows the gradient $m$ and $y$-intercept $c$ immediately.
$y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$Builds a line from a known point $(x_1,y_1)$ and gradient $m$.
$ax+by+c=0$General form; rearrange to $y=mx+c$ when the gradient is needed.
Worked Example Equation from two points

Find the equation of the line through $(1,5)$ and $(2,8)$.

1
Gradient
$m=\dfrac{8-5}{2-1}=3$
2
Substitute
$5 = 3(1)+c \Rightarrow c=2$
$y=3x+2$
Worked Example Parallel line and general form

Find, in the form $ax+by+c=0$, the line through $(3,-1)$ parallel to $2x-y+5=0$.

1
Find the gradient
$2x-y+5=0 \Rightarrow y=2x+5$, so the gradient is $2$. A parallel line also has gradient $2$.
2
Use point-gradient form
$y-(-1)=2(x-3) \Rightarrow y+1=2x-6$.
3
Convert to general form
$y=2x-7 \Rightarrow 2x-y-7=0$.
$2x-y-7=0$
Worked Example Perpendicular line

Find the line through $(2,3)$ perpendicular to $y=2x+7$.

1
Perp. gradient
$m_1 m_2=-1 \Rightarrow m_2 = -\dfrac12$
2
Substitute
$3=-\dfrac12(2)+c \Rightarrow c=4$
$y=-\dfrac12x+4$
Tip — Keywords

Meets the x-axis → set y=0.  Meets the y-axis → set x=0.  Two lines intersect → solve simultaneously.  Asked for an area → sketch everything you're given first.

10. Trigonometry

Right-angle and non-right-angle triangles, radians, and the three trig graphs.
↦ Continues In

P2 assumes the radians, sectors, sine and cosine rules, and trigonometric graphs taught here. In P2 — Trigonometric Identities & Equations, the new content is using the identities $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ and $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$, then solving simple trigonometric equations over a stated interval.

  • Angles in any triangle sum to $180°$.
  • An equilateral triangle has three $60°$ angles and three equal sides.
  • An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal angles.

Right-Angle Triangles

Pythagoras: find a side
$c^2=a^2+b^2$
SOH · CAH · TOA: one side + an angle
$\sin x=\dfrac{opp}{hyp}$
$\cos x=\dfrac{adj}{hyp}$
$\tan x=\dfrac{opp}{adj}$
Inverse ratios: find an angle
$x=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{opp}{hyp}\right)$, and similarly for $\cos^{-1}$, $\tan^{-1}$
Area
$\tfrac12\times \text{base}\times\text{height}$

Non-Right-Angle Triangles

Sine rule
$$\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}$$side: need 2 angles + 1 side · angle: need 2 sides + 1 angle
Cosine rule — side
$$b^2=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B$$needs 2 sides + the included angle
Cosine rule — angle
$$A=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}\right)$$needs all 3 sides

$\tfrac12 ab\sin C$
Worked Example Cosine rule

A triangle has $a=7$, $c=9$, and included angle $B=52°$. Find $b$.

1
Substitute
$b^2 = 7^2+9^2-2(7)(9)\cos52°$
2
Evaluate
$b^2 \approx 52.4267$, so $b\approx\sqrt{52.4267}$.
$b \approx 7.24$ (3 s.f.)

The Ambiguous Sine-Rule Case

When two sides and a non-included angle are known, the sine rule may produce two possible angles because $\sin\theta=\sin(180°-\theta)$. Calculate the acute angle, test its supplement, and reject any option that makes the angle sum at least $180°$.

Worked Example Two possible triangles

In triangle $ABC$, $a=7$, $b=10$ and $A=30°$. Find the possible values of $B$.

1
Apply the sine rule
$\dfrac{\sin B}{10}=\dfrac{\sin30°}{7}$, so $\sin B=\dfrac{5}{7}$.
2
Find the principal angle
$B_1=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac57\right)\approx45.6°$.
3
Test the supplement
$B_2=180°-45.6°=134.4°$. Since $30°+134.4°<180°$, this second triangle is also possible.
$B\approx45.6°$ or $B\approx134.4°$.

Radians & Sectors

Formula

$\pi \text{ rad} = 180°$

Area of sector $=\tfrac12 \theta r^2$     Arc length $=\theta r$  ($\theta$ in radians)

Trig Graphs

Know the shape, period, and key values of all three — they underpin every trig-equation and transformation question.

FunctionPeriodSymmetry
$\sin x$$360°$ or $2\pi$Odd: $\sin(-x)=-\sin x$; rotational symmetry about the origin.
$\cos x$$360°$ or $2\pi$Even: $\cos(-x)=\cos x$; reflection symmetry in the $y$-axis.
$\tan x$$180°$ or $\pi$Odd: $\tan(-x)=-\tan x$; rotational symmetry about the origin.
Interpreting One Transformation

For $y=\sin(2x)$, the factor $2$ acts horizontally: the graph is compressed by scale factor $\frac12$, so its period is $\frac{360°}{2}=180°$. For $y=3\cos x$, the factor $3$ acts vertically, so the amplitude is $3$ while the period remains $360°$.

Unit E · Calculus

11. Differentiation

Derivative as tangent gradient and rate of change · power rule · second derivatives · tangents and normals
↦ Continues In

P2 — Differentiation applies this power rule, including rational powers, to stationary points, increasing and decreasing intervals, and practical maximum or minimum problems. Chain, product and quotient rules, together with trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic derivatives, belong to P3.

Differentiation produces a gradient function. If $y=f(x)$, then $f'(x)$ or $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ gives both the gradient of the tangent and the instantaneous rate of change at a particular value of $x$.

From a Secant to a Tangent

The gradient between two points is the gradient of a secant. As the second point moves closer to the first, the secant approaches the tangent:

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$

This limit explains what a derivative means. P1 questions normally ask you to use the differentiation rules below rather than derive them from first principles.

Prepare the Expression First

Rewrite roots and reciprocal powers in the form $x^n$, expand simple products where needed, and split algebraic fractions into separate powers of $x$. Then apply the power rule term by term.

Rules

Power rule
$y=ax^n \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{dy}{dx}=anx^{n-1}$
Linear term
$y=ax \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{dy}{dx}=a$
Constant term
$y=a \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$
Expand Before Differentiating

P1 does not require the product rule. For example, if $y=(2x-1)(x^2+3)$, first expand to $y=2x^3-x^2+6x-3$, then differentiate to obtain $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=6x^2-2x+6$.

Worked Example

Differentiate $y=\dfrac{5x^2+8x-10}{x}$.

1
Split
$y = 5x+8-10x^{-1}$
2
Differentiate
$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5+0+10x^{-2}$
$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5+\dfrac{10}{x^2}$

Second Derivatives

Differentiate the gradient function again to obtain the second derivative, written $f''(x)$, $y''$ or $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. It measures how the gradient itself is changing.

First and Second Derivatives

$$y=f(x) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=f''(x).$$

Worked Example Second derivative

For $y=2x^3-3x^2+4x-1$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ and $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.

1
Differentiate once
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=6x^2-6x+4$.
2
Differentiate the gradient
$\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=12x-6$.
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=6x^2-6x+4$,   $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=12x-6$

Tangents and Normals

The tangent has the same gradient as the curve at the point of contact. The normal is perpendicular to the tangent at that point.

At the point of contact, the curve and tangent share a gradient; the normal has the negative reciprocal gradient.
Equations Through $(x_1,y_1)$

If $m=f'(x_1)$, then

Tangent: $\quad y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$

Normal: $\quad y-y_1=-\dfrac{1}{m}(x-x_1)$, provided $m\ne0$.

Worked Example Tangent and normal

The curve $y=x^2+2x$ passes through the point where $x=1$. Find the equations of the tangent and normal at that point.

1
Find the point
At $x=1$, $y=1^2+2(1)=3$, so the point is $(1,3)$.
2
Find the tangent gradient
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2x+2$, so at $x=1$, $m_{\text{tangent}}=4$.
3
Write the tangent
$y-3=4(x-1) \Rightarrow y=4x-1$.
4
Write the normal
$m_{\text{normal}}=-\dfrac14$, so $y-3=-\dfrac14(x-1) \Rightarrow y=-\dfrac14x+\dfrac{13}{4}$.
Tangent: $y=4x-1$  ·  Normal: $y=-\dfrac14x+\dfrac{13}{4}$
Special Case

If the tangent gradient is $0$, the tangent is horizontal, $y=y_1$, and the normal is the vertical line $x=x_1$.

Preview — Used in P2

Setting $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$ locates stationary points. P2 develops this into classifying maxima and minima, identifying increasing and decreasing intervals, and solving optimisation problems. To find the gradient at a specific value of $x$, substitute that value directly into $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

12. Integration

Reversing differentiation to recover the original function.
↦ Continues In

P2 — Integration applies the P1 reverse power rule to definite integrals and areas under or between curves. Rational powers are included, but $x^{-1}$ is excluded. Exact integration of trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions is outside P1/P2.

integration undoes differentiation

Rule

Formula

$$\int ax^n\,dx=\dfrac{ax^{n+1}}{n+1}+c,\qquad n\ne-1$$

  1. Add one to the power.
  2. Divide by the new power.
  3. Add the constant of integration, $c$. If a point on the curve is given, use it to determine $c$. P2 introduces definite integrals, where constants cancel when subtracting the endpoint values.
Worked Example

Find $\displaystyle\int \left(2x^2-3x-\dfrac{6}{x^2}\right)dx$.

1
Rewrite
$\int \left(2x^2 - 3x - 6x^{-2}\right)dx$
2
Integrate each term
$\dfrac{2x^3}{3} - \dfrac{3x^2}{2} - \dfrac{6x^{-1}}{-1}$
$\dfrac{2x^3}{3} - \dfrac{3x^2}{2} + \dfrac{6}{x} + c$, for $x\ne0$
Tip

A boundary condition (a point the curve passes through) lets you solve for $c$ — substitute the given $x$ and $y$ into your integrated expression once the integration itself is done.

Worked Example Reconstructing a curve

Given $f'(x)=3x^2-4x+1$ and that the curve $y=f(x)$ passes through $(2,5)$, find $f(x)$.

1
Integrate
$f(x)=\displaystyle\int(3x^2-4x+1)\,dx=x^3-2x^2+x+c$.
2
Use the point
$5=2^3-2(2^2)+2+c=2+c$, so $c=3$.
$f(x)=x^3-2x^2+x+3$
