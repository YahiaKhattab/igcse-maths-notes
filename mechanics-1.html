1. Mathematical Models in Mechanics

The simplifying assumptions behind every mechanics question, and what each one lets you do.

Mechanics questions describe real objects, such as cars, ropes and planks, using a small set of idealised words. Each word is a modelling assumption: it removes a complication and tells you something you can use in the calculation.

Key Facts — Weight and $g$

Learn this. The formulae booklet gives no formulae for M1, so everything in these notes must be known.

$$W = mg \qquad g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$$

Weight is a force, measured in newtons, and it acts vertically downwards. A particle of mass 5 kg has weight $5\times9.8=49$ N.

Modelling Vocabulary

RodIts thickness is ignored and it is rigid, so it does not bend.The forces act at points along a line. Use moments.
Uniform rodIts mass is spread evenly along its length.The weight acts at the midpoint.
Non-uniform rodIts mass is not spread evenly.The weight acts at the centre of mass, which is given or has to be found.
Light (string, rod or pulley)It has no mass.Its weight is ignored. The tension is the same all along a light string.
Inextensible stringIt does not stretch.While the string is taut, the connected particles have the same speed and the same size of acceleration.
Smooth surfaceThere is no friction.The only contact force is the normal reaction.
Rough surfaceFriction acts.Friction opposes the motion, up to a largest value of $\mu R$.
Smooth light pulleyThe pulley has no mass and no friction at its axle.The tension is the same on both sides of the pulley.
PegA fixed support that a string passes over or a rod rests on.For a smooth peg, the tension is the same on both sides of the string.
BeadA particle with a hole through it, threaded on a wire or string.It can only move along the wire or string.

LaminaA flat object whose thickness is ignored.Its mass is spread over an area, not concentrated at a point.

Criticising a Model

Questions sometimes ask how a model could be improved, or how an assumption has been used. Name the assumption and say what it changed.

  • "The string is light" means the tension is the same throughout. A real rope has weight, so the tension would vary along it.
  • "The string is inextensible" means both particles have the same acceleration. A real rope stretches a little.
  • "The ball is a particle" means air resistance and spin are ignored. Including air resistance would be a refinement.
Exam Technique — $g$, Accuracy and Diagrams

The front cover of the WME01 paper tells you to take $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$ whenever a numerical value is needed, and to give your answer to either 2 significant figures or 3 significant figures. The examiners' report adds that more accurate answers lose marks, although exact multiples of $g$ are usually accepted. Start every question with a clear force diagram: one arrow for each force, starting on the object it acts on.

Common Mistake

Mixing up mass and weight. Mass is in kilograms; weight is the force $mg$ in newtons. Use the mass in $F=ma$ and in momentum, and draw the weight $mg$ on the force diagram. Also, do not give four or more significant figures after using $g=9.8$.

2. Vectors in Mechanics

Quantities with size and direction: forces, velocities and positions in $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$ form.
↤ Builds On

P1 — Trigonometry covers $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$ in right-angled triangles. Resolving a vector into components, and finding its direction, uses exactly those ratios.

A vector has a magnitude and a direction; a scalar has a magnitude only. Force, displacement, velocity and acceleration are vectors. The unit vectors $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ are at right angles: usually horizontal and vertical, or due east and due north.

Formula — Vectors

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet). For $\mathbf{a} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j}$:

$$|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \qquad \tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and the $\mathbf{i}$ direction. Going the other way, a vector of magnitude $F$ at angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf{i}$ is $F\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i} + F\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j}$.

$$\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}t \qquad \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t \qquad \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$$

The first is for constant velocity and the second for constant acceleration.

For example, a force of 10 N at $30^\circ$ to $\mathbf{i}$ has components $10\cos30^\circ=8.66$ N in the $\mathbf{i}$ direction and $10\sin30^\circ=5$ N in the $\mathbf{j}$ direction.

Direction: Angle and Bearing

  • The angle with $\mathbf{i}$ comes from $\tan\theta$. Sketch the vector first so that you know which quadrant it is in.
  • A bearing is measured clockwise from north and written with three figures. If $\mathbf{j}$ is north and the vector points north-east, the bearing is $90^\circ - \theta$.

Resultants, Velocity and Acceleration

  • The resultant of several vectors is their sum: add the $\mathbf{i}$ components and add the $\mathbf{j}$ components.
  • Speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector.
  • For constant velocity, $\text{velocity} = \dfrac{\text{change of displacement}}{\text{time}}$. For constant acceleration, $\text{acceleration} = \dfrac{\text{change of velocity}}{\text{time}}$.
Worked Example 1 Resultant, magnitude and direction

Three forces act on a particle of mass 2 kg: $\mathbf{F}_1=(5\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j})$ N, $\mathbf{F}_2=(-2\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})$ N and $\mathbf{F}_3=(3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j})$ N, where $\mathbf{i}$ is due east and $\mathbf{j}$ is due north.

Find (a) the resultant force $\mathbf{R}$, (b) the magnitude of $\mathbf{R}$, (c) the angle $\mathbf{R}$ makes with $\mathbf{i}$, and the bearing of $\mathbf{R}$, (d) the acceleration of the particle.

1
(a) Add the components
$\mathbf{R}=(5-2+3)\mathbf{i}+(2+4-2)\mathbf{j}=6\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}$
2
(b) Magnitude
$|\mathbf{R}|=\sqrt{6^2+4^2}=\sqrt{52}=7.211\ldots$
3
(c) Angle with i
Both components are positive, so $\mathbf{R}$ points north-east. $\tan\theta=\dfrac{4}{6}$, so $\theta=33.69\ldots^\circ$.
4
(c) Bearing
Bearings are measured clockwise from north, which is the $\mathbf{j}$ direction: $90^\circ-33.69\ldots^\circ=56.30\ldots^\circ$.
5
(d) Use F = ma
$\mathbf{a}=\dfrac{\mathbf{R}}{m}=\dfrac{6\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}}{2}=3\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$
(a) $(6\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})$ N  ·  (b) $7.21$ N  ·  (c) $33.7^\circ$ to $\mathbf{i}$, bearing $056^\circ$  ·  (d) $(3\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j})\text{ m s}^{-2}$
Worked Example 1. Drawn nose to tail, the three forces end where the resultant $\mathbf{R}$ ends.

Position Vectors

An object that starts at $\mathbf{r}_0$ and moves with constant velocity $\mathbf{v}$ has position $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+\mathbf{v}t$ at time $t$.

ConditionWhat to write
$A$ is due north of $B$The $\mathbf{i}$ components are equal, and $A$ has the larger $\mathbf{j}$ component.
$A$ is due east of $B$The $\mathbf{j}$ components are equal, and $A$ has the larger $\mathbf{i}$ component.
Distance between $A$ and $B$$|\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A|$
Closest approachWrite $d^2$ as a quadratic in $t$ and complete the square.
Tip

Work with $d^2$, not $d$, when finding the shortest distance. The square root is awkward, and $d$ is smallest exactly when $d^2$ is smallest.

Common Mistake

Giving the angle with $\mathbf{i}$ when the question asks for a bearing, or the other way round. Also, "due north of" needs two checks: equal $\mathbf{i}$ components is not enough, because the object could be due south.

Worked Example 2 Position vector and "due north"

At time $t=0$ a ship $S$ is at the point with position vector $(2\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j})$ km relative to a lighthouse $O$. The ship moves with constant velocity $(3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})\text{ km h}^{-1}$, where $\mathbf{i}$ is due east and $\mathbf{j}$ is due north. A port $P$ has position vector $(14\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j})$ km.

Find (a) the speed of the ship, (b) its position vector at time $t$ hours, (c) the time at which the ship is due north of the port, (d) its distance from the port at that time.

1
(a) Speed
The speed is the magnitude of the velocity: $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\text{ km h}^{-1}$
2
(b) Position at time t
$\mathbf{r}=(2\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j})+(3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j})t=(2+3t)\mathbf{i}+(-5+4t)\mathbf{j}$
3
(c) Equal i components
Due north of $P$ means the same $\mathbf{i}$ component as $P$: $2+3t=14$, so $t=4$.
4
(c) Check the j component
At $t=4$ the $\mathbf{j}$ component is $-5+4(4)=11$, which is greater than 3. So the ship is north of the port, not south.
5
(d) Distance
The ship is directly north, so the distance is the difference of the $\mathbf{j}$ components: $11-3=8$ km.
(a) $5\text{ km h}^{-1}$  ·  (b) $(2+3t)\mathbf{i}+(-5+4t)\mathbf{j}$ km  ·  (c) $t=4$ hours  ·  (d) $8$ km

Worked Example 3 Closest approach of two ships

At time $t=0$ ship $A$ is at $(-4\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j})$ km and ship $B$ is at $(8\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})$ km. Ship $A$ has constant velocity $(6\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j})\text{ km h}^{-1}$ and ship $B$ has constant velocity $(2\mathbf{i}+5\mathbf{j})\text{ km h}^{-1}$.

(a) Find the position vectors of $A$ and $B$ at time $t$ hours. (b) Show that the distance $d$ km between the ships satisfies $d^2=25t^2-120t+160$. (c) Find the shortest distance between the ships and the time at which it occurs.

1
(a) Position vectors
$\mathbf{r}_A=(-4+6t)\mathbf{i}+(3+2t)\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{r}_B=(8+2t)\mathbf{i}+(-1+5t)\mathbf{j}$
2
(b) Vector from A to B
$\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A=(12-4t)\mathbf{i}+(-4+3t)\mathbf{j}$
3
(b) Square the distance
$d^2=(12-4t)^2+(-4+3t)^2=144-96t+16t^2+16-24t+9t^2=25t^2-120t+160$
4
(c) Complete the square
$25t^2-120t+160=25(t^2-4.8t)+160=25(t-2.4)^2-144+160=25(t-2.4)^2+16$
5
(c) Read off the minimum
The squared bracket is never negative, so the least value of $d^2$ is 16, when $t=2.4$. The shortest distance is $\sqrt{16}=4$ km.
Shortest distance $4$ km, at $t=2.4$ hours (2 hours 24 minutes)

3. Kinematics in a Straight Line

Constant acceleration in a straight line: five equations, a sign convention and four kinds of graph.
Displacement, $s$
Distance from the start in a chosen direction. It can be negative.
Distance
The total length of the path travelled. It is never negative.
Velocity, $v$
Rate of change of displacement. Its sign shows the direction.
Speed
The magnitude of the velocity.
Formula — Constant Acceleration

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet). With initial velocity $u$, final velocity $v$, acceleration $a$, time $t$ and displacement $s$:

$$v = u + at \qquad s = \tfrac12(u+v)t$$

$$s = ut + \tfrac12at^2 \qquad s = vt - \tfrac12at^2$$

$$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$

These equations only work while the acceleration is constant.

EquationQuantity it leaves out
$v = u + at$$s$
$s = \tfrac12(u+v)t$$a$
$s = ut + \tfrac12at^2$$v$
$s = vt - \tfrac12at^2$$u$
$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$t$
  1. Choose a positive direction and keep it for the whole stage.
  2. List $s$, $u$, $v$, $a$, $t$, filling in what you know. A deceleration is a negative acceleration.
  3. Pick the equation that contains your three known values and the one you want.
  4. If the acceleration changes, start a new list. The final velocity of one stage is the initial velocity of the next.
Worked Example 4 Two stages

A car passes a point $A$ with speed $6\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and accelerates at $2\text{ m s}^{-2}$ for 5 seconds, reaching a point $B$. At $B$ the driver brakes, and the car slows down uniformly, coming to rest at a point $C$, where $BC=64$ m.

Find (a) the speed of the car at $B$, (b) the distance $AB$, (c) the deceleration from $B$ to $C$, (d) the average speed for the journey from $A$ to $C$.

1
(a) Speed at B
From $A$ to $B$: $u=6$, $a=2$, $t=5$. Using $v=u+at$: $v=6+2(5)=16$
2
(b) Distance AB
Using $s=ut+\tfrac12at^2$: $s=6(5)+\tfrac12(2)(5^2)=55$
3
(c) Deceleration
From $B$ to $C$: $u=16$, $v=0$, $s=64$. Using $v^2=u^2+2as$: $0=16^2+2a(64)$, so $a=-2$. The deceleration is $2\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
4
(d) Time from B to C
Using $v=u+at$: $0=16-2t$, so $t=8$.
5
(d) Average speed
The total distance is $55+64=119$ m and the total time is $5+8=13$ s, so the average speed is $\dfrac{119}{13}=9.153\ldots$
(a) $16\text{ m s}^{-1}$  ·  (b) $55$ m  ·  (c) $2\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (d) $9.15\text{ m s}^{-1}$ (3 s.f.)

Vertical Motion Under Gravity

If air resistance is ignored, an object moving freely up or down has acceleration $g$ downwards for the whole of its flight.

  • Taking upwards as positive, $a=-9.8$.
  • At the greatest height, $v=0$.
  • The object passes each height twice: once going up and once coming down, with the same speed both times.
  • When it returns to its starting level, $s=0$.
Worked Example 5 A ball thrown upwards

A ball is projected vertically upwards from a point $O$ with speed $21\text{ m s}^{-1}$. The ball is modelled as a particle moving freely under gravity.

Find (a) the greatest height above $O$ reached by the ball, (b) the times at which the ball is 10 m above $O$, (c) the speed of the ball when it returns to $O$.

1
(a) Greatest height
Take upwards as positive: $u=21$, $a=-9.8$, and $v=0$ at the top. Using $v^2=u^2+2as$: $0=21^2-2(9.8)h$, so $h=\dfrac{441}{19.6}=22.5$
2
(b) Set up a quadratic
Using $s=ut+\tfrac12at^2$ with $s=10$: $10=21t-4.9t^2$, so $4.9t^2-21t+10=0$
3
(b) Solve
$t=\dfrac{21\pm\sqrt{21^2-4(4.9)(10)}}{2(4.9)}=\dfrac{21\pm\sqrt{245}}{9.8}$, so $t=0.5456\ldots$ (going up) or $t=3.740\ldots$ (coming down).
4
(c) Back at O
The displacement is zero: $v^2=21^2+2(-9.8)(0)=441$, so the speed is $21\text{ m s}^{-1}$, the same as the speed of projection.
(a) $22.5$ m  ·  (b) $t=0.546$ s and $t=3.74$ s (3 s.f.)  ·  (c) $21\text{ m s}^{-1}$

Worked Example 6 Velocity–time graph

A train starts from rest at station $A$ and accelerates uniformly for 20 s until its speed is $24\text{ m s}^{-1}$. It travels at this speed for $T$ seconds, then decelerates uniformly at $0.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$ and comes to rest at station $B$. The distance $AB$ is 3000 m.

(a) Sketch a velocity–time graph for the journey. (b) Find the acceleration in the first stage. (c) Find the value of $T$ and the total time for the journey.

1
(a) Sketch
The graph is a trapezium: a straight line up to 24, a horizontal line, then a straight line down to 0. It is shown below with the values found in this solution.
2
(b) Gradient
The acceleration is the gradient of the first line: $\dfrac{24}{20}=1.2\text{ m s}^{-2}$
3
(c) Time to stop
The speed falls from 24 to 0 at $0.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$, so the last stage takes $\dfrac{24}{0.8}=30$ s.
4
(c) Use the area
The area under the graph is the distance. The two triangles have areas $\tfrac12(20)(24)=240$ and $\tfrac12(30)(24)=360$, and the rectangle has area $24T$.
5
(c) Solve for T
$240+24T+360=3000$, so $24T=2400$ and $T=100$. The total time is $20+100+30=150$ s.
(b) $1.2\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (c) $T=100$, total time $150$ s
Worked Example 6. The three areas add up to the distance of 3000 m.
Common Mistake

Using one set of constant-acceleration equations across a change of acceleration. Each stage needs its own list of $s$, $u$, $v$, $a$, $t$. Also watch the signs: with upwards positive, the acceleration due to gravity is $-9.8$, even while the object is moving up.

Worked Example 7 One vehicle catches another

A van $P$ travels along a straight road at a constant speed of $12\text{ m s}^{-1}$. At time $t=0$ the van passes a point $O$. At the same instant a motorcycle $Q$ starts from rest at a point 32 m behind $O$ and moves in the same direction with constant acceleration $2\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

Find (a) the time at which $Q$ catches $P$, (b) the distance from $O$ at which this happens, (c) the speed of $Q$ at that instant.

1
Displacements from O
At time $t$, the van is at $12t$. The motorcycle starts 32 m behind $O$, so it is at $\tfrac12(2)t^2-32=t^2-32$.
2
(a) Set them equal
$t^2-32=12t$, so $t^2-12t-32=0$
3
(a) Solve
$t=\dfrac{12\pm\sqrt{144+128}}{2}=6\pm\sqrt{68}$, so $t=14.246\ldots$ or $t=-2.246\ldots$ The time must be positive, so reject the negative root.
4
(b) Distance from O
Use the van: $12\times14.246\ldots=170.95\ldots$
5
(c) Speed of Q
Using $v=u+at$: $2\times14.246\ldots=28.49\ldots$
(a) $t=14.2$ s  ·  (b) $171$ m  ·  (c) $28.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ (3 s.f.)

4. Dynamics: Newton's Laws

Forces cause acceleration: Newton's laws, friction, slopes and connected particles.
  1. First law. A particle stays at rest, or keeps moving with constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.
  2. Second law. The resultant force equals mass times acceleration, and the acceleration is in the direction of the resultant force.
  3. Third law. If $A$ exerts a force on $B$, then $B$ exerts an equal and opposite force on $A$.
Formula — Force and Motion

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$F = ma \qquad W = mg$$

$F$ is the resultant force in the direction of the acceleration. In vector form, $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$.

$$F \leq \mu R \qquad F = \mu R \text{ when sliding or about to slide}$$

Here $F$ is the friction, $R$ is the normal reaction and $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction.

The Forces You Will Meet

Weight
$mg$, vertically downwards.
Normal reaction, $R$
At right angles to the surface, pushing the object away from it.
Tension, $T$
The pull in a taut string or rod, acting along it.
Thrust
The push in a rod that is being compressed.
Friction, $F$
Along the surface, opposing the motion.
Resistance
Any force opposing the motion, such as air resistance.
  1. Draw a force diagram and mark the direction of the acceleration.
  2. At right angles to the motion there is no acceleration, so those forces balance. This usually gives $R$.
  3. Along the direction of motion, write resultant force $=ma$.
Worked Example 8 Driving force and resistance

A van of mass 1200 kg moves along a straight horizontal road. The engine produces a constant driving force of 2100 N and the total resistance to motion is a constant 600 N.

(a) Find the acceleration of the van. (b) The van starts from rest. Find the time it takes to reach $20\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and the distance it travels in that time. (c) At $20\text{ m s}^{-1}$ the engine is switched off. Find how much further the van travels before coming to rest.

1
(a) F = ma
The resultant force forwards is the driving force minus the resistance: $2100-600=1200a$, so $a=1.25\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
2
(b) Time
Using $v=u+at$: $20=0+1.25t$, so $t=16$ s.
3
(b) Distance
Using $s=\tfrac12(u+v)t$: $s=\tfrac12(0+20)(16)=160$ m.
4
(c) New acceleration
With the engine off, the only horizontal force is the resistance: $-600=1200a$, so $a=-0.5\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
5
(c) Stopping distance
Using $v^2=u^2+2as$: $0=20^2+2(-0.5)s$, so $s=400$ m.
(a) $1.25\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (b) $16$ s, $160$ m  ·  (c) $400$ m
Worked Example 9 A lift

A lift of mass 650 kg carries a passenger of mass 70 kg. The lift is raised by a vertical cable.

(a) The lift accelerates upwards at $1.2\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Find the tension in the cable and the normal reaction between the passenger and the floor. (b) Later, still moving upwards, the lift slows down at $1.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Find the new normal reaction on the passenger.

1
(a) Whole system
The cable supports the lift and the passenger together, a total mass of $650+70=720$ kg. Taking upwards as positive: $T-720g=720(1.2)$, so $T=720(9.8+1.2)=7920$ N.
2
(a) Passenger alone
Only two forces act on the passenger, the reaction $R$ up and the weight $70g$ down: $R-70g=70(1.2)$, so $R=70(9.8+1.2)=770$ N.
3
(b) Slowing down
The lift is moving up but the acceleration is downwards, so $a=-1.8$: $R-70g=70(-1.8)$, so $R=70(9.8-1.8)=560$ N.
(a) $T=7920$ N, $R=770$ N  ·  (b) $R=560$ N
Tip

Find $R$ before you use friction. $R$ equals $mg$ only on a horizontal surface with no other vertical forces. In a lift, on a slope, or when a force acts at an angle, it is different.

Inclined Planes

↤ Builds On

P1 — Trigonometry gives the ratios used to split the weight into two components on a slope.

On a plane inclined at angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal, resolve parallel and perpendicular to the plane, not horizontally and vertically. The motion is along the plane, so only one direction has an acceleration.

Weight component down the plane
$mg\sin\alpha$
Weight component into the plane
$mg\cos\alpha$
Worked Example 10 Pulled up a rough plane

A particle of mass 4 kg is pulled up a rough plane by a force of 40 N acting parallel to the plane. The plane is inclined at $30^\circ$ to the horizontal and the coefficient of friction between the particle and the plane is $0.25$. Find the acceleration of the particle.

1
Perpendicular to the plane
There is no acceleration in this direction, so $R=4g\cos30^\circ=33.94\ldots$ N.
2
Friction
The particle is moving, so friction is at its largest value: $F=0.25R=8.487\ldots$ N. It acts down the plane, opposing the motion.
3
Parallel to the plane
The weight component down the plane is $4g\sin30^\circ=19.6$ N. Taking up the plane as positive: $40-19.6-8.487\ldots=4a$
4
Solve
$11.91\ldots=4a$, so $a=2.978\ldots$
$a=2.98\text{ m s}^{-2}$ (3 s.f.), up the plane
Worked Example 10. The slope is drawn at $30^\circ$; $R$ is at right angles to it and the weight is vertical.
Common Mistake

Writing $R=mg$ on a slope. On an inclined plane $R=mg\cos\alpha$, unless another force also has a component perpendicular to the plane. Also check the direction of friction: it opposes the motion, so it acts down the plane when the particle moves up.

Connected Particles

↤ Builds On

P1 — Simultaneous Equations covers elimination. Two connected particles give two equations in the acceleration and the tension, and adding them removes the tension.

  • Particles joined by a taut, inextensible string have the same speed and the same size of acceleration.
  • If they move along the same straight line (a car and trailer), you can treat them as one object to find the acceleration. The tension is an internal force and cancels.
  • To find the tension, look at one particle on its own.
  • If the string passes over a pulley, the particles move in different directions. Write $F=ma$ for each particle, then add the equations.
  • The force on a pulley is the resultant of the two tensions. With both parts of the string vertical it is $2T$; with the parts at right angles it is $T\sqrt2$.
Worked Example 11 Car and trailer

A car of mass 1100 kg tows a trailer of mass 400 kg along a straight horizontal road, using a light horizontal tow bar. The driving force of the car is 3000 N. The resistances to motion are 400 N on the car and 200 N on the trailer.

(a) Find the acceleration. (b) Find the tension in the tow bar. (c) When the speed is $12\text{ m s}^{-1}$ the tow bar is detached. Find how far the trailer travels before it comes to rest, and the time this takes.

1
(a) Whole system
The car and trailer move together, so treat them as one object of mass 1500 kg: $3000-400-200=1500a$, so $a=1.6\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
2
(b) Trailer alone
The forces on the trailer are the tension forwards and its resistance backwards: $T-200=400(1.6)$, so $T=840$ N.
3
(b) Check with the car
$3000-400-840=1100(1.6)$, and both sides equal 1760.
4
(c) Trailer after detaching
The only horizontal force is now the resistance: $-200=400a$, so $a=-0.5\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
5
(c) Distance and time
$0=12^2+2(-0.5)s$, so $s=144$ m. Then $0=12-0.5t$, so $t=24$ s.
(a) $1.6\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (b) $840$ N  ·  (c) $144$ m in $24$ s
Worked Example 12 Two particles over a pulley

Particles of mass 3 kg and 5 kg are attached to the ends of a light inextensible string, which passes over a smooth fixed pulley. The particles hang with the string taut and vertical, and are released from rest.

Find (a) the acceleration of the particles, (b) the tension in the string, (c) the force exerted on the pulley by the string.

1
Equation for each particle
The 5 kg particle moves down and the 3 kg particle moves up, with the same acceleration $a$: $5g-T=5a$ and $T-3g=3a$.
2
(a) Add the equations
The tension cancels: $2g=8a$, so $a=\dfrac g4=2.45\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
3
(b) Tension
From the 3 kg particle: $T=3g+3(2.45)=36.75$ N.
4
(c) Force on the pulley
Both parts of the string pull down on the pulley with force $T$: $2T=73.5$ N, vertically downwards.
(a) $2.45\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (b) $36.8$ N (3 s.f.)  ·  (c) $73.5$ N downwards
Worked Example 12. The tension is the same on both sides because the pulley is smooth and the string is light.

When the Forces Change

If a string breaks or goes slack, or a particle hits the ground, the forces change and so does the acceleration. Treat the motion as two stages.

  • The speed at the end of the first stage is the initial speed of the second stage.
  • Draw a new force diagram for the second stage. A slack string has no tension.
  • Check any limit the question gives, such as a particle not reaching the pulley.
Worked Example 13 Table, pulley and floor

A particle $A$ of mass 2 kg rests on a rough horizontal table, 2.5 m from a smooth pulley fixed at the edge of the table. The coefficient of friction between $A$ and the table is $0.4$. A light inextensible string passes over the pulley and joins $A$ to a particle $B$ of mass 3 kg, which hangs 0.9 m above the floor. The system is released from rest with the string taut.

Find (a) the acceleration of the particles, (b) the tension in the string, (c) how much further $A$ moves after $B$ hits the floor, given that $B$ does not rebound.

1
Friction on A
Vertically for $A$: $R=2g=19.6$ N. $A$ is moving, so $F=0.4(19.6)=7.84$ N.
2
Equation for each particle
For $B$, moving down: $3g-T=3a$. For $A$, moving towards the pulley: $T-7.84=2a$.
3
(a) Add the equations
$29.4-7.84=5a$, so $5a=21.56$ and $a=4.312\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
4
(b) Tension
$T=7.84+2(4.312)=16.464$ N.
5
(c) Speed when B lands
Both particles have moved 0.9 m from rest: $v^2=2(4.312)(0.9)=7.7616$, so $v=2.785\ldots\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
6
(c) A on its own
The string is now slack, so the only horizontal force on $A$ is friction: $-7.84=2a$, so $a=-3.92\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
7
(c) Further distance
$0=7.7616+2(-3.92)s$, so $s=0.99$ m. In total $A$ moves $0.9+0.99=1.89$ m, which is less than 2.5 m, so it stops before reaching the pulley.
(a) $4.31\text{ m s}^{-2}$  ·  (b) $16.5$ N  ·  (c) $0.99$ m
Worked Example 13, drawn to scale: $A$ starts 2.5 m from the pulley and $B$ starts 0.9 m above the floor.
Common Mistake

Assuming the tension equals the weight of the hanging particle. That is only true if the system is at rest or moving at constant speed. When it accelerates, find $T$ from $F=ma$. Also, do not keep using the first acceleration after the string has gone slack.

5. Momentum & Impulse

What a collision conserves, and how a force acting for a time changes the motion.

The momentum of a particle is its mass times its velocity. An impulse is what changes momentum: a force acting for a time. Both are vectors, so in one dimension their signs show their directions.

Formula — Momentum and Impulse

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$\text{momentum} = mv \qquad I = Ft = mv - mu$$

$$m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$$

The second line is the principle of conservation of momentum for two particles that collide directly. Momentum and impulse are both measured in N s.

  1. Draw a "before" and an "after" diagram, with an arrow for each velocity.
  2. Choose a positive direction. A velocity in the other direction is negative.
  3. Write total momentum before $=$ total momentum after.
  4. For an impulse, use $I=mv-mu$ on one particle. The two particles receive equal and opposite impulses.
Tip

If you do not know which way a particle moves after a collision, draw its arrow in the positive direction and call its velocity $v$. A negative answer simply means it moves the other way.

Worked Example 14 Collision with a change of direction

A particle $P$ of mass 2 kg is moving at $6\text{ m s}^{-1}$ on a smooth horizontal surface. It collides directly with a particle $Q$ of mass 3 kg, which is moving towards it at $2\text{ m s}^{-1}$. After the collision the direction of motion of $P$ is reversed and its speed is $1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

Find (a) the speed and direction of $Q$ after the collision, (b) the magnitude of the impulse exerted on $Q$ in the collision.

1
Signs
Take the original direction of $P$ as positive. Before: $P$ has velocity $6$ and $Q$ has velocity $-2$. After: $P$ has velocity $-1.5$ and $Q$ has velocity $v$.
2
(a) Conservation of momentum
$2(6)+3(-2)=2(-1.5)+3v$, so $6=-3+3v$ and $v=3$. This is positive, so $Q$ now moves in the original direction of $P$: its direction has been reversed too.
3
(b) Impulse on Q
$I=mv-mu=3(3-(-2))=15$ N s.
4
(b) Check with P
The impulse on $P$ is $2(-1.5-6)=-15$ N s: the same size, in the opposite direction.
(a) $3\text{ m s}^{-1}$, in the original direction of $P$  ·  (b) $15$ N s
Worked Example 14. Arrow lengths are proportional to the speeds; both particles reverse direction.
Common Mistake

Losing a minus sign when a particle changes direction. A particle that goes from $6\text{ m s}^{-1}$ one way to $1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ the other way has changed its velocity by 7.5, not 4.5. Write every velocity with its sign before substituting.

Worked Example 15 Impulse of a constant force

A ball of mass 0.16 kg is moving in a straight line at $5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ when it is struck by a bat. The bat exerts a constant force of 48 N on the ball for 0.05 s, in the direction opposite to the ball's motion. Find the impulse on the ball and its speed immediately after it is struck.

1
Impulse
$I=Ft=48\times0.05=2.4$ N s.
2
Signs
Take the direction of the force as positive. The ball was moving the other way, so $u=-5$.
3
Impulse and momentum
$I=mv-mu$ gives $2.4=0.16(v-(-5))$, so $v+5=15$ and $v=10$.
Impulse $2.4$ N s  ·  speed $10\text{ m s}^{-1}$, in the direction of the force

A String That Becomes Taut

When a slack string between two particles suddenly becomes taut, there is a jerk: an impulse in the string. Afterwards the particles move with a common speed. Momentum is conserved, and the impulse in the string is the change in momentum of either particle.

Worked Example 16 The jerk in a string

Particles $A$ of mass 2 kg and $B$ of mass 3 kg lie at rest on a smooth horizontal table, joined by a light inextensible string which is slack. $B$ is projected directly away from $A$ with speed $5\text{ m s}^{-1}$. Find the common speed of the particles just after the string becomes taut, and the magnitude of the impulse in the string.

1
Conservation of momentum
Before the jerk only $B$ is moving. Afterwards both particles move together with speed $v$: $3(5)=(2+3)v$, so $v=3\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
2
Impulse on A
$A$ goes from rest to $3\text{ m s}^{-1}$: $I=2(3)-2(0)=6$ N s.
3
Check with B
$B$ loses momentum $3(5)-3(3)=6$ N s, the same amount.
Common speed $3\text{ m s}^{-1}$  ·  impulse $6$ N s
Unit C · Equilibrium

6. Statics of a Particle

Forces in balance: resolving in two directions, limiting friction and the triangle of forces.
↤ Builds On

P1 — Trigonometry covers the sine and cosine rules, which solve a triangle of forces that is not right-angled.

A particle is in equilibrium when the resultant force on it is zero. It is then at rest, or moving with constant velocity.

Formula — Equilibrium

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$\text{resultant force} = 0$$

So the components in any direction add up to zero. Resolve in two perpendicular directions to get two equations.

$$F \leq \mu R \qquad F = \mu R \text{ in limiting equilibrium}$$

  1. Draw a force diagram showing every force on the particle.
  2. Choose two perpendicular directions. On a slope, use parallel and perpendicular to the plane.
  3. In each direction, write forces one way $=$ forces the other way.
  4. Solve the two equations.

The Triangle of Forces

Three forces in equilibrium, drawn nose to tail, form a closed triangle, because their vector sum is zero. The triangle can then be solved with trigonometry. If it is right-angled, use Pythagoras and $\tan$. If not, use the sine rule (learn it) or the cosine rule (given in the formulae booklet under P1).

Worked Example 17 Three forces

A particle of weight 24 N hangs from a light string. A horizontal force of 10 N holds the particle in equilibrium with the string at an angle $\theta$ to the vertical. Find $\theta$ and the tension $T$ in the string.

1
Resolve horizontally
The horizontal component of the tension balances the 10 N force: $T\sin\theta=10$
2
Resolve vertically
The vertical component of the tension balances the weight: $T\cos\theta=24$
3
Divide
$\tan\theta=\dfrac{10}{24}$, so $\theta=22.61\ldots^\circ$
4
Tension
Square and add the two equations: $T=\sqrt{10^2+24^2}=26$ N. The triangle of forces gives the same result at once, as its hypotenuse.
$\theta=22.6^\circ$ (3 s.f.)  ·  $T=26$ N
Worked Example 17. The forces are drawn to scale, and the angle $\theta$ is the same in both pictures.

Friction and Limiting Equilibrium

While a particle stays at rest, friction is only as large as it needs to be, up to a largest value of $\mu R$. The words limiting equilibrium, on the point of moving and about to slip all mean that friction has reached that largest value, so $F=\mu R$.

Tip

Friction opposes the motion that is about to happen. Decide which way the particle would move if there were no friction, then draw the friction arrow the other way.

Worked Example 18 Finding the coefficient of friction

A box of mass 5 kg rests on a rough horizontal floor. It is pulled by a rope inclined at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. When the tension in the rope is 20 N the box is on the point of sliding. Find the coefficient of friction between the box and the floor.

1
Resolve vertically
The rope lifts the box slightly, so the reaction is less than the weight: $R+20\sin30^\circ=5g$, so $R=49-10=39$ N.
2
Resolve horizontally
Friction balances the horizontal pull: $F=20\cos30^\circ=17.32\ldots$ N.
3
Limiting friction
The box is about to slide, so $F=\mu R$: $\mu=\dfrac{17.32\ldots}{39}=0.4441\ldots$
$\mu=0.44$ (2 s.f.)

A Range of Values

On a rough slope a particle can be in equilibrium for a whole range of values of a force. At one end of the range it is about to slip down, with friction acting up the plane. At the other end it is about to move up, with friction acting down the plane.

Worked Example 19 Held on a slope by a horizontal force

A particle of mass 2 kg rests on a rough plane inclined at an angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal, where $\tan\alpha=\dfrac{3}{4}$. The coefficient of friction is $0.5$. A horizontal force of magnitude $P$ newtons acts on the particle, towards the plane, in the vertical plane containing a line of greatest slope. Find the range of values of $P$ for which the particle stays in equilibrium.

1
Set up
$\tan\alpha=\dfrac{3}{4}$ gives $\sin\alpha=0.6$ and $\cos\alpha=0.8$. The weight is $2g=19.6$ N, with components $19.6(0.8)=15.68$ N into the plane and $19.6(0.6)=11.76$ N down the plane.
2
Perpendicular to the plane
$P$ has a component $0.6P$ into the plane, so in both cases $R=15.68+0.6P$.
3
About to slip down
Friction $0.5R$ acts up the plane, helping $P$: $0.8P+0.5(15.68+0.6P)=11.76$. So $0.8P+7.84+0.3P=11.76$, giving $1.1P=3.92$ and $P=3.563\ldots$
4
About to move up
Friction $0.5R$ now acts down the plane: $0.8P=11.76+0.5(15.68+0.6P)$. So $0.8P-0.3P=11.76+7.84$, giving $0.5P=19.6$ and $P=39.2$
$3.56\leq P\leq39.2$ (3 s.f.)
Common Mistake

Using $F=\mu R$ when the particle is not on the point of moving. In ordinary equilibrium, friction is found by resolving, and all you know is $F\leq\mu R$. Also remember that a force at an angle changes $R$: in the example above, $R$ includes $0.6P$.

Unit D · Moments

7. Moments

The turning effect of a force, and the two conditions that keep a rod in equilibrium.

A force can turn a rigid body as well as move it. The moment of a force about a point measures that turning effect.

Formula — Moments

Learn this (not in the formulae booklet):

$$\text{moment} = F \times d$$

$d$ is the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force. Moments are measured in N m and are either clockwise or anticlockwise. A force of 6 N acting 0.5 m from a pivot has moment $6\times0.5=3$ N m.

For a rigid body in equilibrium:

$$\text{resultant force} = 0 \qquad \text{clockwise moments} = \text{anticlockwise moments}$$

The moments balance about any point.

SituationWhat it tells you
Uniform rodThe weight acts at the midpoint.
Non-uniform rodThe weight acts at the centre of mass. Call its distance from one end $x$.
Rod resting on a supportThe support pushes up with a normal reaction.
Rod hanging from a stringThe string pulls up with a tension.
On the point of tilting about a supportThe reaction at the other support is zero.
Tip

Take moments about a point where an unknown force acts. That force has no moment about its own point, so it drops out of the equation. Then resolve vertically to find it.

Worked Example 20 Two supports

A uniform rod $AB$ has length 6 m and mass 20 kg. It rests horizontally on two supports, at $C$ and $D$, where $AC=1$ m and $AD=5$ m. A load of mass 12 kg is placed on the rod at the point 4.5 m from $A$. Find the reactions at $C$ and at $D$.

1
Distances from C
The weight acts at the midpoint, 3 m from $A$, so $3-1=2$ m from $C$. The load is $4.5-1=3.5$ m from $C$, and $D$ is $5-1=4$ m from $C$.
2
Moments about C
The reaction at $C$ has no moment about $C$: $4R_D=20g(2)+12g(3.5)=82g$
3
Reaction at D
$R_D=20.5g=200.9$ N.
4
Resolve vertically
$R_C+R_D=32g$, so $R_C=11.5g=112.7$ N.
$R_C=113$ N  ·  $R_D=201$ N (3 s.f.)
Worked Example 20. Distances are to scale.
Worked Example 21 Centre of mass of a non-uniform rod

A non-uniform rod $AB$ has length 3 m and mass 8 kg. It hangs in equilibrium in a horizontal position from two vertical strings, attached at $A$ and at $B$. The tension in the string at $B$ is 30 N. Find the tension in the string at $A$ and the distance of the centre of mass of the rod from $A$.

1
Resolve vertically
The weight is $8g=78.4$ N. The two tensions support it: $T_A+30=78.4$, so $T_A=48.4$ N.
2
Moments about A
Let the centre of mass be $x$ metres from $A$. The tension at $A$ has no moment about $A$: $78.4x=30(3)$
3
Solve
$x=1.147\ldots$
$T_A=48.4$ N  ·  centre of mass $1.15$ m from $A$ (3 s.f.)

Worked Example 22 On the point of tilting

A uniform plank $AB$ has length 5 m and mass 30 kg. It rests horizontally on two supports, at $C$ and $D$, where $AC=1$ m and $AD=3.5$ m. A child of mass 40 kg stands on the plank at $D$ and walks slowly towards $B$. Find how far beyond $D$ the child can walk before the plank tilts, and the reaction at $D$ at that instant.

1
Tilting condition
When the plank is about to tilt about $D$, it is just losing contact with $C$, so the reaction at $C$ is zero.
2
Distances from D
The weight of the plank acts at the midpoint, 2.5 m from $A$, which is $3.5-2.5=1$ m from $D$, on the side of $C$. Let the child be $x$ metres beyond $D$.
3
Moments about D
$30g(1)=40g(x)$, so $x=0.75$. The child is then $3.5+0.75=4.25$ m from $A$, which is still on the plank.
4
Resolve vertically
With no reaction at $C$, the support at $D$ carries everything: $R_D=70g=686$ N.
The child can walk $0.75$ m beyond $D$  ·  $R_D=686$ N
Worked Example 22. About to tilt about $D$: the plank's weight and the child's weight balance on either side of $D$.
Common Mistake

Leaving out the weight of the rod, or measuring a distance from the end of the rod when taking moments about a support. Every distance in a moments equation is measured from the point you are taking moments about.

Worked Example 23 One reaction twice the other

A uniform plank $AB$ has length 4 m and mass 10 kg. It rests horizontally on supports at its ends $A$ and $B$. A load of mass 20 kg is placed on the plank at a distance $x$ metres from $A$. The reaction at $A$ is twice the reaction at $B$. Find the two reactions and the value of $x$.

1
Resolve vertically
$R_A+R_B=30g$. Since $R_A=2R_B$, this gives $3R_B=30g$, so $R_B=10g=98$ N and $R_A=20g=196$ N.
2
Moments about A
The plank's weight acts 2 m from $A$ and the reaction at $B$ acts 4 m from $A$: $10g(2)+20g(x)=10g(4)$
3
Solve
Dividing by $10g$: $2+2x=4$, so $x=1$.
$R_A=196$ N, $R_B=98$ N  ·  $x=1$ m
Worked Example 23. The load sits nearer to $A$, the support with the larger reaction.
