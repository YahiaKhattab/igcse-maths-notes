Unit 1 · Algebraic Methods

1. Factor & Remainder Theorem

Polynomial division · testing factors · factorising cubics · the remainder theorem

A polynomial is an expression made up of terms with non-negative whole-number powers of $x$, such as $3x^3+5x^2+3x+5$. Polynomials of degree 3 (cubics) and degree 4 (quartics) appear frequently in P2 — you need to be able to divide them, test them for factors, and fully factorise them.

The Factor Theorem

Formula — Factor Theorem

If $f(x)$ is a polynomial and $f(a)=0$, then $(x-a)$ is a factor of $f(x)$.

More generally, if $f\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=0$, then $(bx-a)$ is a factor of $f(x)$.

This works both ways: if $(x-a)$ is a factor, substituting $x=a$ into $f(x)$ must give zero. This gives a quick way to test whether a linear expression is a factor of a polynomial, without doing any division at all.

Worked Example Testing a factor

Show that $(x-2)$ is a factor of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12$.

1
Identify the root to test
Since the factor is $(x-2)$, we substitute $x=2$.
2
Substitute
$f(2) = (2)^3 - 3(2)^2 - 4(2) + 12 = 8 - 12 - 8 + 12 = 0$
3
Conclude
Since $f(2)=0$, by the factor theorem $(x-2)$ is a factor of $f(x)$.
✓ $(x-2)$ is confirmed as a factor
Tip — Which values to try

When you need to find a factor from scratch (rather than test a given one), try factors of the constant term first — for $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-4x+12$, try $x = \pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm4,\pm6,\pm12$. If the leading coefficient isn't 1, also consider fractions $\dfrac{p}{q}$ where $p$ divides the constant term and $q$ divides the leading coefficient.

The Remainder Theorem

Formula — Remainder Theorem

If a polynomial $f(x)$ is divided by $(x-a)$, the remainder is $f(a)$.

If $f(x)$ is divided by $(bx-a)$, the remainder is $f\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)$.

The factor theorem is really just a special case of the remainder theorem: a factor is simply a divisor that leaves a remainder of zero.

Worked Example Finding a remainder

Find the remainder when $f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 5x + 3$ is divided by $(x+2)$.

1
Write the divisor as $(x-a)$
$(x+2) = (x-(-2))$, so $a=-2$.
2
Evaluate $f(a)$
$f(-2) = 2(-2)^3+(-2)^2-5(-2)+3 = -16+4+10+3=1$
Remainder = 1

Fully Factorising a Cubic

Once you've found one linear factor using the factor theorem, divide the cubic by that factor to leave a quadratic, then factorise (or use the quadratic formula on) the quadratic to find the remaining factors.

Method A — Algebraic Long Division

Divide the cubic by the known factor term-by-term, exactly like long division of numbers, keeping track of place value in powers of $x$.

Method B — Comparing Coefficients

Write $f(x) = (x-a)(px^2+qx+r)$, expand the right-hand side, and match coefficients of $x^3$, $x^2$, $x$ and the constant term to find $p$, $q$, $r$.

Worked Example Full factorisation

Fully factorise $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12$.

1
Find a first factor
From the earlier example, $f(2)=0$, so $(x-2)$ is a factor.
2
Compare coefficients
Let $f(x) = (x-2)(x^2+bx+c)$. Expanding: $x^3+(b-2)x^2+(c-2b)x-2c$.
Comparing constants: $-2c=12 \Rightarrow c=-6$.
Comparing $x^2$: $b-2=-3 \Rightarrow b=-1$.
Check the $x$ coefficient: $c-2b=-6+2=-4$, as required.
3
Write the quadratic factor
$f(x) = (x-2)(x^2-x-6)$
4
Factorise the quadratic
$x^2-x-6 = (x-3)(x+2)$
$f(x) = (x-2)(x-3)(x+2)$
Common Mistake

Always check your final factorisation by expanding it back out, or by testing one more root. A sign error in the comparing-coefficients step is the most common cause of lost marks in this topic.

Sketching from a Factorised Cubic

Once fully factorised, $f(x) = (x-2)(x-3)(x+2)$ tells you immediately that the curve crosses the $x$-axis at $x=2,\ 3,\ -2$, and crosses the $y$-axis at $f(0) = (-2)(-3)(2) = 12$. Since the coefficient of $x^3$ is positive, the graph starts low on the left and rises to the right, alternating direction at each root.

2. The Binomial Expansion

Pascal's triangle · $\binom{n}{r}$ notation · expanding $(a+b)^n$ · finding a specific term

The binomial expansion gives a shortcut for expanding brackets of the form $(a+b)^n$ for a positive integer $n$, without multiplying everything out by hand.

Binomial Coefficients

Formula — Binomial Coefficient

$$\binom{n}{r} = {}^nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$$

This counts the number of ways of choosing $r$ items from $n$, and gives the coefficients in the expansion of $(a+b)^n$.

Each row gives the coefficients for $(a+b)^n$. Each entry is the sum of the two entries diagonally above it.

Pascal's triangle is quick for small $n$, but for larger $n$ (or when you only need one term), use $\binom{n}{r}$ directly.

The General Expansion

Formula — Binomial Expansion

$$(a+b)^n = a^n + \binom{n}{1}a^{n-1}b + \binom{n}{2}a^{n-2}b^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r + \dots + b^n$$

The general term is $\binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r$, and there are $n+1$ terms in total.

Worked Example Full expansion

Expand $(2+3x)^4$ in ascending powers of $x$.

1
Set up with $a=2$, $b=3x$, $n=4$
Coefficients from row $n=4$ of Pascal's triangle: $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$.
2
Write each term
$1\cdot 2^4 + 4\cdot 2^3(3x) + 6\cdot 2^2(3x)^2 + 4\cdot 2^1(3x)^3 + 1\cdot(3x)^4$
3
Simplify each term
$16 + 4(8)(3x) + 6(4)(9x^2) + 4(2)(27x^3) + 81x^4$
$= 16 + 96x + 216x^2 + 216x^3 + 81x^4$
$(2+3x)^4 = 16 + 96x + 216x^2 + 216x^3 + 81x^4$
Common Mistake

Don't forget to raise both $a$ and $b$ to the appropriate powers — a very common error is expanding $(2+3x)^4$ as if it were $(1+3x)^4$, forgetting that $2^{n-r}$ needs to be included in every term, not just the first.

Finding a Specific Term

You often don't need the whole expansion — just one particular term, such as the coefficient of $x^3$. Use the general term formula directly.

Worked Example One term only

Find the coefficient of $x^3$ in the expansion of $(1-2x)^{7}$.

1
Write the general term
With $a=1,\ b=-2x,\ n=7$: general term $=\binom{7}{r}(1)^{7-r}(-2x)^r = \binom{7}{r}(-2)^r x^r$
2
Set the power equal to 3
We need $r=3$: $\binom{7}{3}(-2)^3 = 35 \times (-8) = -280$
Coefficient of $x^3$ is $-280$
Tip — Using expansions for estimates

Binomial expansions can approximate numerical values. For example, substituting a small value of $x$ into $(1+x)^n$ and its expansion gives an approximation for $(1+x)^n$ using only the first few terms — useful when a full calculation would be impractical.

Unit 2 · Coordinate Geometry

3. Circles

Equation of a circle · centre and radius · tangents · circle theorems
↤ Builds On

Finding a circle's centre and radius reuses P1 — Quadratics (completing the square), and tangent problems reuse P1 — The Discriminant (setting $D=0$).

The Equation of a Circle

Formula — Circle centred at $(a,b)$

$$(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$$

This is the set of all points a fixed distance $r$ (the radius) from the centre $(a,b)$ — it comes directly from the distance formula.

Every point $(x,y)$ on the circle is a distance $r$ from the centre $(a,b)$.

General Form of a Circle's Equation

Formula — Expanded (general) form

$$x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+c=0$$

has centre $(-g,\,-f)$ and radius $r=\sqrt{g^2+f^2-c}$.

This form arises from expanding $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$. To go from the general form back to centre-radius form, complete the square on the $x$ terms and the $y$ terms separately.

Worked Example Completing the square

Find the centre and radius of the circle $x^2+y^2-6x+4y-3=0$.

1
Group $x$ and $y$ terms
$(x^2-6x) + (y^2+4y) = 3$
2
Complete the square on each bracket
$(x-3)^2-9 + (y+2)^2-4 = 3$
3
Rearrange to circle form
$(x-3)^2+(y+2)^2 = 3+9+4 = 16$
Centre $(3,-2)$, radius $r=4$

Key Circle Theorems

Tip — The three circle facts you'll use constantly

1. The angle subtended by a diameter at the circumference is always $90°$.
2. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord (and vice versa).
3. A tangent to a circle is always perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

Worked Example Using the tangent property

A circle has equation $(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2=25$. Find the equation of the tangent to the circle at the point $(4,6)$.

1
Find the gradient of the radius
Centre $(1,2)$ to $(4,6)$: gradient $=\dfrac{6-2}{4-1}=\dfrac{4}{3}$
2
Use the perpendicular gradient
Tangent $\perp$ radius, so tangent gradient $=-\dfrac{3}{4}$
3
Use $y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$ through $(4,6)$
$y-6=-\dfrac{3}{4}(x-4) \Rightarrow 4y-24=-3x+12 \Rightarrow 3x+4y=36$
$3x+4y=36$
Common Mistake

Remember to check a point genuinely lies on the circle before using it as a tangent point — substitute it into the circle's equation first if it isn't already stated to be on the circle.

Unit 3 · Exponentials & Logarithms

4. Exponentials & Logarithms

General graphs $y=a^x$ · growth and decay · logarithm laws · solving $a^x=b$

The General Exponential Function

An exponential function has the variable in the power:

General Form and Conditions

$$y=a^x, \qquad a\gt0, \quad a\ne1.$$

The restriction $a\gt0$ keeps the function real for every real $x$. The value $a=1$ is excluded because $1^x=1$ is constant rather than exponential growth or decay.

Every valid $y=a^x$ graph passes through $(0,1)$ and approaches, but never reaches, the $x$-axis.
BaseBehaviourShared graph facts
$a\gt1$Increasing: exponential growthDomain: all real $x$
Range: $y\gt0$
$y$-intercept: $(0,1)$
Horizontal asymptote: $y=0$
$0\lt a\lt1$Decreasing: exponential decay
Worked Example Reading an exponential graph

For $y=3^x$, state the $y$-intercept, horizontal asymptote, range and whether the function is increasing or decreasing.

1
Use $x=0$
$3^0=1$, so the graph crosses the $y$-axis at $(0,1)$.
2
Use the base
Since $3\gt1$, the function is increasing. Its outputs are always positive and approach $0$ as $x$ becomes very negative.
$y$-intercept $(0,1)$; asymptote $y=0$; range $y\gt0$; increasing.

Logarithms and the Natural Base

A logarithm reverses an exponential: $a^x=b$ means $x=\log_a b$. The special base $e\approx2.71828$ gives the natural logarithm.

Inverse Relationships

$$\log_a(a^x)=x, \qquad a^{\log_a x}=x, \qquad \ln x=\log_e x.$$

In logarithms, the base must satisfy $a\gt0$, $a\ne1$, and the argument must be positive.

Laws of Logarithms

Formula — Laws of Logs

$$\log_a x + \log_a y = \log_a(xy)$$ $$\log_a x - \log_a y = \log_a\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)$$ $$\log_a(x^n) = n\log_a x$$ $$\log_a a = 1 \qquad \log_a 1 = 0$$

Note — Change of Base

$$\log_a x = \frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}$$ Use this to evaluate logs on a calculator in a base it doesn't directly support, or to compare logs with different bases.

Worked Example Simplifying logs

Write $2\ln 5 - \ln 10$ as a single logarithm.

1
Apply the power law
$2\ln 5 = \ln(5^2)=\ln 25$
2
Apply the subtraction law
$\ln 25 - \ln 10 = \ln\left(\dfrac{25}{10}\right) = \ln 2.5$
$\ln 2.5$

Solving Exponential & Log Equations

Tip — Two standard approaches

Exponential equation (unknown in the power): take logarithms of both sides using any consistent base.
Log equation (unknown inside a log): combine into a single log, then raise $e$ (or the relevant base) to both sides.

Worked Example Exponential equation

Solve $3^{2x-1}=20$, giving your answer to 3 significant figures.

1
Take logs of both sides
$\log\left(3^{2x-1}\right)=\log20 \Rightarrow (2x-1)\log3=\log20$.
2
Solve for $x$
$2x-1=\dfrac{\log20}{\log3} \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{1+\frac{\log20}{\log3}}{2}=1.863\ldots$.
$x = 1.86$ (3 s.f.)
Worked Example Logarithmic equation

Solve $\ln(x+3) - \ln x = 2$, $x>0$.

1
Combine into a single log
$\ln\left(\dfrac{x+3}{x}\right)=2$
2
Remove the log by raising $e$ to both sides
$\dfrac{x+3}{x}=e^2$
3
Solve for $x$
$x+3=e^2x \Rightarrow 3 = x(e^2-1) \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{3}{e^2-1}$
$x \approx 0.470$ (3 s.f.)
Common Mistake

Always check log solutions are valid — you cannot take the log of a negative number or zero. If solving a quadratic in $\ln x$ or $e^x$ produces two roots, one may need to be rejected.

Exponential Growth & Decay

A model of the form $y=Aa^t$ has initial value $A$ because $a^0=1$. It represents growth when $a\gt1$ and decay when $0\lt a\lt1$. Models written as $Ae^{kt}$ are the same general idea with base $e$: $k\gt0$ gives growth and $k\lt0$ gives decay.

5. Trigonometric Identities & Equations

P1 recap: radians, rules and graphs · P2 content: identities and equations over stated intervals
↤ Builds On

P1 — Trigonometry supplies the radian measure, arc and sector formulae, sine and cosine rules, and base trigonometric graphs assumed in P2. The new P2 work is applying two identities and solving simple trigonometric equations over a stated interval.

Radians

Formula — Radians

$$\pi \text{ radians} = 180°$$

To convert degrees $\to$ radians, multiply by $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$. To convert radians $\to$ degrees, multiply by $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$.

Degrees0°30°45°60°90°180°360°
Radians$0$$\frac{\pi}{6}$$\frac{\pi}{4}$$\frac{\pi}{3}$$\frac{\pi}{2}$$\pi$$2\pi$
Tip — Why radians?

Radians make the arc length and sector area formulae especially simple. They also make later calculus with trigonometric functions work cleanly: for example, $\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)=\cos x$ is valid when $x$ is measured in radians, a fact studied in P3. In P2 questions, always follow the angle unit and interval stated.

Arc Length & Sector Area

A sector of radius $r$ and angle $\theta$ (in radians), with arc length $s$.
Formula — Sectors (θ in radians)

Arc length: $$s=r\theta$$

Sector area: $$A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$$

Segment area (sector minus triangle): $$A_{\text{segment}}=\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta-\sin\theta)$$

Worked Example Sector problem

A sector has radius $8\text{ cm}$ and angle $1.2$ radians. Find its arc length and area.

1
Arc length
$s = r\theta = 8 \times 1.2 = 9.6\text{ cm}$
2
Area
$A=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}(64)(1.2)=38.4\text{ cm}^2$
Arc length $=9.6$ cm, Area $=38.4$ cm²

The Sine Rule & Cosine Rule

For a general (non right-angled) triangle with sides $a,b,c$ opposite angles $A,B,C$:

Formula — Sine Rule

$$\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}$$

Use when you know a matching angle/side pair, plus one more side or angle.

Formula — Cosine Rule

$$a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A$$

Rearranged to find an angle: $$\cos A = \frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}$$

Use when you know all three sides (SSS), or two sides and the included angle (SAS).

Formula — Area of a Triangle

$$A=\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$$

Common Mistake — The Ambiguous Case

When using the sine rule to find an angle given two sides and a non-included angle (SSA), there can be two valid solutions — one acute and one obtuse ($180°-\theta$). Always check whether the obtuse case makes sense given the context.

$y=\cos x$ — same shape as $\sin x$, shifted left by $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
$y=\tan x$ — period $\pi$, with vertical asymptotes wherever $\cos x = 0$.

Trig Identities

Formula — Core Identities

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$$

$$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$$

The identity $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ comes directly from Pythagoras' theorem applied to a right-angled triangle in the unit circle, and is the single most useful trig identity in P2 — it lets you rewrite equations that mix $\sin$ and $\cos$ (or $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$) entirely in terms of one function.

Solving Trig Equations

Tip — General method

1. Rearrange to isolate the trig function.
2. Find the principal value using inverse trig ($\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$, $\tan^{-1}$).
3. Use symmetry of the graph (or the CAST diagram) to find all other solutions inside the given interval.

Worked Example Solving over an interval

Solve $\sin\theta = 0.6$ for $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, giving answers to 3 s.f.

1
Find the principal value
$\theta = \sin^{-1}(0.6) = 0.6435\ldots$
2
Find the second solution
Since $\sin$ is positive in the first and second quadrants: $\theta = \pi - 0.6435 = 2.498\ldots$
$\theta = 0.644$ or $\theta = 2.50$ (3 s.f.)
Common Mistake

Make sure your calculator is in the correct mode (radians vs. degrees) to match the question, and always check whether all found solutions actually lie within the given interval before writing your final answer.

Unit 5 · Calculus

6. Differentiation

Stationary points · maxima and minima · increasing and decreasing functions · optimisation
↤ Builds On

This topic applies the power rule and gradient skills from P1 — Differentiation, then uses second derivatives to classify stationary points. P2 does not introduce chain, product or quotient rules, or derivatives of trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions; those are P3 topics.

In P2, the differentiation rules remain the polynomial rules learned in P1. The new skill is using those rules to analyse a function: locate and classify stationary points, decide where a function is increasing or decreasing, and solve practical maximum or minimum problems.

Stationary Points

A stationary point occurs where the gradient is zero, so solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$. Then find the corresponding $y$-coordinate and classify the point.

Second Derivative Test

At a stationary point $x=a$:

If $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\big|_{x=a}>0$, the point is a local minimum.

If $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\big|_{x=a}<0$, the point is a local maximum.

If $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\big|_{x=a}=0$, the test is inconclusive; inspect the sign of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ on either side.

Worked Example Stationary points and intervals

For $y=x^3-6x^2+9x+2$, find and classify the stationary points. Hence state the intervals on which the function is increasing.

1
Differentiate and set the gradient to zero
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3)=0$, so $x=1$ or $x=3$.
2
Find the coordinates
$y(1)=6$ and $y(3)=2$, so the stationary points are $(1,6)$ and $(3,2)$.
3
Use the second derivative
$\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=6x-12$. At $x=1$ it is $-6$, so $(1,6)$ is a local maximum. At $x=3$ it is $6$, so $(3,2)$ is a local minimum.
4
Use the sign of the derivative
$3(x-1)(x-3)>0$ outside the roots, so the function is increasing for $x<1$ and $x>3$.
Maximum at $(1,6)$; minimum at $(3,2)$; increasing for $x<1$ and $x>3$.

Increasing and Decreasing Functions

Gradient Sign Test

If $\dfrac{dy}{dx}>0$ throughout an interval, the function is increasing there.

If $\dfrac{dy}{dx}<0$ throughout an interval, the function is decreasing there.

Use the roots of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$ to split the number line into intervals, then test or factorise the derivative to determine its sign.

Exam Language

When asked to show that a function is increasing, a graph alone is not sufficient. Differentiate and demonstrate that $\dfrac{dy}{dx}>0$ over the required interval.

Practical Maximum and Minimum Problems

Worked Example Optimisation

A rectangle has perimeter $40$ cm. Find the dimensions that give the greatest possible area.

1
Create a one-variable model
Let the side lengths be $x$ and $y$. Since $2x+2y=40$, $y=20-x$. Therefore $A=x(20-x)=20x-x^2$, where $0<x<20$.
2
Find the stationary value
$\dfrac{dA}{dx}=20-2x=0$, so $x=10$ and $y=10$.
3
Confirm it is a maximum
$\dfrac{d^2A}{dx^2}=-2<0$, so the stationary value is a maximum. The maximum area is $10\times10=100$ cm$^2$.
The rectangle must be $10$ cm by $10$ cm; maximum area $=100$ cm$^2$.

7. Integration

Definite integrals · area under a curve · area between two curves
↤ Builds On

This topic builds on P1 — Integration, which covers the reverse power rule for polynomials.

P2 uses the polynomial integration rules from P1 to evaluate definite integrals and calculate areas. Integrals involving trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions are P3 material and are not required here.

Polynomial Integration Recap

P1 Rule Used in P2
$f(x)$$\int f(x)\,dx$
$x^n\ (n\ne-1)$$\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+c$

Apply the rule term by term after expanding brackets or simplifying the expression where necessary.

Common Mistake

Never forget the constant of integration $+c$ for an indefinite integral — it disappears only in a definite integral, where the two limits cause it to cancel.

Definite Integrals & Area

$\displaystyle A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ gives the area between the curve and the $x$-axis, from $x=a$ to $x=b$.
Formula — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b)-F(a)$$ where $F(x)$ is any antiderivative of $f(x)$.

Worked Example Definite integral / area

Find the area between the curve $y=6x-x^2$, the $x$-axis, and the lines $x=0$ and $x=6$.

1
Set up and integrate
$A=\displaystyle\int_0^6 (6x-x^2)\,dx=\left[3x^2-\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^6$.
2
Substitute the limits
$A=\left(3(6)^2-\dfrac{6^3}{3}\right)-0=108-72=36$.
Area $=36$ square units

Area Between Two Curves

Upper Curve Minus Lower Curve

If $y=f(x)$ is above $y=g(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$, then

$$A=\int_a^b \big(f(x)-g(x)\big)\,dx.$$

When the curves exchange positions, split the integral at their intersection.

Worked Example Area between curves

Find the finite area enclosed by $y=4x$ and $y=x^2$.

1
Find the intersections
$4x=x^2 \Rightarrow x(x-4)=0$, so the curves meet at $x=0$ and $x=4$.
2
Identify the upper curve
For $0<x<4$, $4x>x^2$, so subtract $x^2$ from $4x$.
3
Integrate the difference
$A=\displaystyle\int_0^4(4x-x^2)\,dx=\left[2x^2-\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^4=32-\dfrac{64}{3}=\dfrac{32}{3}$.
Area $=\dfrac{32}{3}$ square units
Common Mistake — Areas below the axis

If a curve dips below the $x$-axis over part of the interval, the definite integral there comes out negative. If you want the actual (positive) area, split the integral at the root(s) and take the absolute value of each part separately before adding.

8. The Trapezium Rule

Numerical integration · estimating areas · over- and under-estimates

Some functions can't be integrated using standard techniques (or an exact integral simply isn't needed). The trapezium rule approximates the area under a curve by splitting it into equal-width strips and treating each strip as a trapezium rather than a true curved region.

Each strip is treated as a trapezium using the two $y$-values at its edges.
Formula — Trapezium Rule

$$\int_a^b y\,dx \approx \frac{h}{2}\Big[(y_0+y_n) + 2(y_1+y_2+\dots+y_{n-1})\Big]$$

where $h=\dfrac{b-a}{n}$ is the strip width, and $y_0,y_1,\ldots,y_n$ are the $y$-values at $n+1$ equally spaced $x$-values from $a$ to $b$.

Tip — Reading it in words

"Half the strip width, times (the first $y$ plus the last $y$, plus twice everything in between)." The first and last ordinates are only counted once each — every ordinate in between is shared by two trapezia, so it's counted twice.

Worked Example Applying the trapezium rule

Use the trapezium rule with 4 strips to estimate $\displaystyle\int_0^2 e^{x^2}\,dx$, using the table of values below.

1
Find the strip width
$h=\dfrac{2-0}{4}=0.5$
2
Substitute into the formula
$\int_0^2 e^{x^2}dx \approx \dfrac{0.5}{2}\Big[(1.000+54.598)+2(1.284+2.718+9.488)\Big]$
3
Evaluate
$=0.25\big[55.598 + 2(13.490)\big]=0.25(55.598+26.980)=0.25(82.578)=20.6$
$\displaystyle\int_0^2 e^{x^2}\,dx \approx 20.6$ (3 s.f.)

Over-estimates and Under-estimates

Curve is convex (∪-shaped)

The straight-line tops of the trapezia sit above the curve, so the trapezium rule gives an over-estimate of the true area.

Curve is concave (∩-shaped)

The straight-line tops sit below the curve, so the trapezium rule gives an under-estimate of the true area.

Common Mistake

Using more strips (a smaller $h$) generally improves the estimate's accuracy — but it does not change whether the estimate is an over- or under-estimate when the curve keeps the same concavity throughout the interval.

Comparing Estimates and Calculating Error

If an exact value $I$ is known, the absolute error is $|T-I|$, where $T$ is the trapezium estimate. The percentage error is $\dfrac{|T-I|}{|I|}\times100\%$.

Worked Example More strips and numerical error

Estimate $\displaystyle\int_0^2x^2\,dx$ using (a) 2 strips and (b) 4 strips. Compare each estimate with the exact value.

1
Use 2 strips
$h=1$ and the ordinates are $0,1,4$. Hence $T_2=\dfrac12[0+4+2(1)]=3$.
2
Use 4 strips
$h=0.5$ and the ordinates are $0,0.25,1,2.25,4$. Hence $T_4=\dfrac{0.5}{2}[0+4+2(0.25+1+2.25)]=2.75$.
3
Find the exact value
$I=\left[\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\dfrac83$.
4
Calculate the errors
$|T_2-I|=\dfrac13$ and $|T_4-I|=\dfrac1{12}$. The percentage errors are $12.5\%$ and $3.125\%$ respectively.
$T_2=3$, $T_4=2.75$; using 4 strips reduces the absolute error from $\frac13$ to $\frac1{12}$.
Unit 6 · Sequences & Series

9. Sequences & Arithmetic Series

nth-term and recurrence definitions · increasing, decreasing and periodic sequences · arithmetic series

Describing a Sequence

A sequence is an ordered list of terms $u_1,u_2,u_3,\ldots$. P2 uses two main descriptions:

DescriptionWhat it givesExample
nth-term formulaFinds a term directly from its position $n$$u_n=3n+1$
Recurrence relationUses one or more earlier terms; an initial value must also be given$u_1=3$, $u_{n+1}=2u_n-1$
A Recurrence Needs a Starting Value

The rule $u_{n+1}=f(u_n)$ alone does not define one unique sequence. Always look for the given first term, such as $u_1=3$.

Worked Example Using a recurrence relation

A sequence is defined by $u_1=3$ and $u_{n+1}=2u_n-1$. Find $u_2,u_3,u_4$ and $u_5$.

1
Substitute the previous term
$u_2=2(3)-1=5$, then $u_3=2(5)-1=9$.
2
Continue in order
$u_4=2(9)-1=17$ and $u_5=2(17)-1=33$.
The first five terms are $3,5,9,17,33$.

Increasing, Decreasing and Periodic Sequences

TypeTestExample
Increasing$u_{n+1}\gt u_n$ for every relevant $n$$2,5,8,11,\ldots$
Decreasing$u_{n+1}\lt u_n$ for every relevant $n$$10,7,4,1,\ldots$
Periodic$u_{n+k}=u_n$ for a fixed positive integer $k$$2,4,2,4,\ldots$ has period $2$

The smallest positive value of $k$ that repeats the sequence is called its period. A sequence that changes direction is not increasing or decreasing throughout, even if some consecutive terms rise or fall.

Worked Example Recognising periodic behaviour

A sequence is defined by $u_1=2$ and $u_{n+1}=6-u_n$. Write the first six terms and describe the sequence.

1
Generate the terms
$u_2=6-2=4$, $u_3=6-4=2$, and the same two values then repeat.
2
Classify the sequence
The first six terms are $2,4,2,4,2,4$. Since $u_{n+2}=u_n$, the sequence is periodic with period $2$.
$2,4,2,4,2,4$; periodic with period $2$.

Arithmetic Sequences

An arithmetic sequence is one where each term is found by adding a fixed amount — the common difference, $d$ — to the previous term. For example: $3,\,7,\,11,\,15,\,\ldots$ has first term $a=3$ and common difference $d=4$.

The nth Term

Formula — nth Term of an Arithmetic Sequence

$$u_n = a + (n-1)d$$

where $a$ is the first term and $d$ is the common difference.

Worked Example Finding a term

An arithmetic sequence has first term $5$ and common difference $-3$. Find the 20th term.

1
Substitute into the formula
$u_{20} = 5 + (20-1)(-3) = 5 - 57 = -52$
$u_{20} = -52$

Sum of an Arithmetic Series

Formula — Sum of the first $n$ terms

$$S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big[2a+(n-1)d\big] = \frac{n}{2}(a+l)$$

where $l$ is the last (nth) term. The second form is useful whenever the last term is already known.

Tip — Where this formula comes from

Write the sum forwards and backwards:

$$\begin{aligned} S_n&=a+(a+d)+\cdots+[a+(n-1)d],\\ S_n&=[a+(n-1)d]+[a+(n-2)d]+\cdots+a. \end{aligned}$$

Adding corresponding terms gives $2S_n=n[2a+(n-1)d]$, so

$$S_n=\frac n2[2a+(n-1)d]=\frac n2(a+l).$$

Sum of the First $n$ Natural Numbers

For $1+2+\cdots+n$, take $a=1$, $d=1$ and last term $l=n$:

$$\sum_{r=1}^{n}r=\frac n2(1+n)=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$

Worked Example Sum of a series

Find the sum of the first 30 terms of the arithmetic sequence $8, 11, 14, \ldots$

1
Identify $a$, $d$, $n$
$a=8$, $d=3$, $n=30$
2
Substitute into $S_n$
$S_{30}=\dfrac{30}{2}\big[2(8)+(29)(3)\big] = 15\big[16+87\big]=15(103)$
$S_{30} = 1545$
Common Mistake

The coefficient of $d$ inside the brackets is $(n-1)$, not $n$ — a very common slip is writing $2a+nd$ instead of $2a+(n-1)d$.

Solving Problems with Simultaneous Equations

Many exam questions give you two pieces of information (e.g. two known terms, or a term and a sum) and ask you to find $a$ and $d$. Set up two equations using the formulas above and solve simultaneously.

Worked Example Finding a and d

In an arithmetic sequence, $u_5=17$ and $u_{12}=45$. Find $a$ and $d$.

1
Write two equations
$a+4d=17 \quad (1)$
$a+11d=45 \quad (2)$
2
Subtract (1) from (2)
$7d=28 \Rightarrow d=4$
3
Substitute back
$a+4(4)=17 \Rightarrow a=1$
$a=1,\ d=4$

10. Geometric Sequences

nth term · common ratio · sum of finite series · sum to infinity

A geometric sequence is one where each term is found by multiplying the previous term by a fixed value — the common ratio, $r$. For example: $2,\,6,\,18,\,54,\ldots$ has first term $a=2$ and common ratio $r=3$.

The nth Term

Formula — nth Term of a Geometric Sequence

$$u_n = ar^{n-1}$$

Worked Example Finding a term

A geometric sequence has first term $4$ and common ratio $\frac12$. Find the 8th term.

1
Substitute into the formula
$u_8 = 4\left(\dfrac12\right)^7 = 4 \times \dfrac{1}{128} = \dfrac{1}{32}$
$u_8=\dfrac{1}{32}$

Sum of a Geometric Series

Formula — Sum of the first $n$ terms

$$S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r} = \frac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}$$

Both forms are equivalent — use whichever avoids a negative denominator (the first when $r<1$, the second when $r>1$).

Proof of the Finite Geometric Sum

Let $S_n=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{n-1}$. Multiplying by $r$ gives $rS_n=ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^n$.

Subtracting, $S_n-rS_n=a-ar^n$, so $S_n(1-r)=a(1-r^n)$. Therefore

$$S_n=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r},\qquad r\ne1.$$

Worked Example Sum of a series

Find the sum of the first 10 terms of $3, 6, 12, 24,\ldots$

1
Identify $a$, $r$, $n$
$a=3$, $r=2$, $n=10$
2
Substitute (using the $r>1$ form)
$S_{10}=\dfrac{3(2^{10}-1)}{2-1} = 3(1024-1)=3(1023)$
$S_{10}=3069$
Worked Example Using logarithms to find $n$

A geometric series has first term $5$ and common ratio $1.3$. Find the least number of terms whose sum exceeds $500$.

1
Form the inequality
$\dfrac{5(1.3^n-1)}{1.3-1}>500$, so $1.3^n>31$.
2
Take logarithms
$n\log(1.3)>\log31$. Since $\log(1.3)>0$, the inequality direction is unchanged.
3
Find the least integer
$n>\dfrac{\log31}{\log1.3}\approx13.09$, so the least possible integer is $14$.
4
Check the boundary
$S_{13}\approx488.1<500$ and $S_{14}\approx639.6>500$.
The least number of terms is $14$.

Sum to Infinity

When $|r|<1$, each term shrinks toward zero, and the running total converges to a finite limit $S_\infty$.
Formula — Sum to Infinity

$$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}, \qquad \text{valid only when } |r|<1$$

Common Mistake

$S_\infty$ only exists when $-1<r<1$. If $|r|\ge1$, the series diverges (grows without bound, or oscillates) and has no sum to infinity — always state the condition on $r$ when using this formula.

Worked Example Sum to infinity

Find $S_\infty$ for the geometric series $20 + 10 + 5 + 2.5 + \ldots$

1
Identify $a$ and $r$
$a=20$, $r=\dfrac{10}{20}=0.5$ (and $|0.5|<1$, so the sum to infinity exists)
2
Substitute
$S_\infty=\dfrac{20}{1-0.5}=\dfrac{20}{0.5}=40$
$S_\infty = 40$

11. Sigma Notation

Reading $\sum$ notation · evaluating series · writing series in sigma form

Sigma notation, $\sum$, is a compact way of writing the sum of a sequence of terms without listing every one out.

Formula — Sigma Notation

$$\sum_{r=1}^{n} u_r = u_1+u_2+u_3+\dots+u_n$$

The variable under $\sum$ (here $r$) is the counter, running from the lower limit to the upper limit inclusive.

Worked Example Evaluating a sum

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{5}(2r+1)$.

1
List the terms
$r=1:3,\ r=2:5,\ r=3:7,\ r=4:9,\ r=5:11$
2
Add them
$3+5+7+9+11=35$
$\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{5}(2r+1) = 35$

Sigma Notation for Arithmetic & Geometric Series

Because $\sum$ is just shorthand for "add these terms up," any series written in sigma notation with a linear expression in $r$ (like $2r+1$ above) is an arithmetic series, and can be summed directly using $S_n=\dfrac{n}{2}(a+l)$ instead of adding term-by-term — this is much faster for large $n$.

Similarly, a sigma sum with $r$ appearing in an exponent (like $3^r$ or $2\times5^r$) is a geometric series, and can be summed with $S_n=\dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$.

Worked Example Recognising a geometric sum

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{6} 3(2)^{r-1}$.

1
Recognise the series type
This is geometric: $a=3$ (the $r=1$ term), $r_{\text{ratio}}=2$, $n=6$ terms.
2
Apply the geometric sum formula
$S_6 = \dfrac{3(2^6-1)}{2-1}=3(63)$
$\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{6} 3(2)^{r-1} = 189$
Tip — Splitting sums

$\sum$ distributes over addition and constant multiples: $$\sum_{r=1}^n \big(au_r + bv_r\big) = a\sum_{r=1}^n u_r + b\sum_{r=1}^n v_r$$ This lets you break a complicated sum into simpler arithmetic/geometric pieces.

Common Mistake

Double-check the number of terms being summed: $\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}$ has $n$ terms, but $\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}$ has $n+1$ terms (since it includes $r=0$). Miscounting the number of terms is a very common way to get $S_n$ formulas wrong.

12. Methods of Proof

Proof by deduction · proof by exhaustion · disproof by counter-example

A mathematical proof is a watertight logical argument that establishes a statement is true for every case it claims to cover — not just a few examples. P2 introduces several standard proof techniques.

Proof by Deduction

Idea — Direct / Deductive Proof

Start from known facts or definitions, and use logical algebraic steps to arrive at the statement you want to prove — true for a general case, not a specific number.

Tip — Represent generality with algebra

To prove something about "any even number," write it as $2n$ (for integer $n$) rather than picking a specific even number like $4$ or $10$. This is what makes the proof general rather than just an example.

Worked Example Proof by deduction

Prove that the sum of two consecutive odd numbers is always divisible by 4.

1
Represent the general case algebraically
Let the two consecutive odd numbers be $2n+1$ and $2n+3$, for integer $n$.
2
Add them
$(2n+1)+(2n+3) = 4n+4 = 4(n+1)$
3
Conclude
Since $n+1$ is an integer, $4(n+1)$ is a multiple of 4. So the sum of two consecutive odd numbers is always divisible by 4. $\blacksquare$

Proof by Exhaustion

Idea — Proof by Exhaustion

Break the statement into a finite number of cases, and check that each case individually holds true. This only works when the possibilities can be fully listed — it does not work for infinite sets of numbers.

Worked Example Proof by exhaustion

Prove that $n^2+n$ is even for every integer $n$.

1
Identify the finite cases
Every integer $n$ is either even or odd — just two cases to check.
2
Case 1: $n$ even
Let $n=2k$. Then $n^2+n = 4k^2+2k = 2(2k^2+k)$, which is even.
3
Case 2: $n$ odd
Let $n=2k+1$. Then $n^2+n = (2k+1)^2+(2k+1) = 4k^2+4k+1+2k+1 = 4k^2+6k+2 = 2(2k^2+3k+1)$, which is even.
4
Conclude
Both possible cases give an even result, so $n^2+n$ is even for all integers $n$. $\blacksquare$

Disproof by Counter-Example

Idea — Disproving a Statement

To show a general statement is false, you only need to find one example where it fails. A single valid counter-example is enough to disprove a claim entirely — no algebra required.

Worked Example Counter-example

Disprove the statement: "$n^2+n+41$ is prime for all positive integers $n$."

1
Test a value
Try $n=41$: $41^2+41+41 = 41(41+1+1)=41(43)$
2
Conclude
This factorises as $41\times43$, so it is not prime. The statement is false — $n=41$ is a counter-example. $\blacksquare$
Common Mistake

Checking a statement for several specific numbers (e.g. $n=1,2,3$) and finding it works is never a valid proof — it only shows the statement holds for those particular cases. Only proof by exhaustion (checking every possible case) or a general algebraic argument counts as a genuine proof.
